AI Foundations — Day 17: Decision Trees and Ensembles

Day 17: Decision Trees and Ensembles Objective: By the end of today, you should understand how decision trees make predictions, why they are easy to interpret, and how ensembles combine many simple models into a stronger one. Prerequisites: You should already be comfortable with basic supervised learning, training vs. test data, evaluation metrics, cross-validation, and the idea that different models have different strengths and limits. 1) Decision trees: if-this-then-that prediction A decision tree is a model that makes predictions by asking a sequence of questions. Each question splits the data into smaller groups. A path from the top of the tree to a final answer is like a set of rules. For example: If income > 50k , go left. If debt 60k . Count how many approved and denied cases fall on each side of the split. Try a second split inside one branch. Compare your tree to a “majority vote” baseline: if most applicants are approved, what would happen if you predicted the majority class every time? Notice how the tree can beat the baseline by using feature-based rules. Also notice how cross-validation thinking applies even in this tiny exercise: if you changed a few examples, would your split still look sensible? Spaced review From Day 12: Always choose evaluation metrics that match the real goal. A tree with high accuracy may still be a bad model if false positives or false negatives matter more. From Day 14: Use cross-validation to compare a single tree against an ensemble fairly. A one-time train/test split can be noisy. From Day 15: Keep a baseline in mind. If a tree or forest cannot clearly beat the simplest useful benchmark, it may not be worth the added complexity. Knowledge check 1) What is the main difference between a single decision tree and an ensemble? 2) Why can a single decision tree overfit? 3) In your own words, what does bagging do? ------------------------------ Answers 1) A single decision tree makes predictions on its own, while an ensemble combines multiple models so their votes or averages produce the final prediction. 2) A tree can overfit because it keeps splitting until it learns patterns that are specific to the training data, including noise and small quirks that do not generalize. 3) Bagging trains many models on different bootstrap samples of the data and then combines their predictions, usually by voting or averaging, to make the result more stable.
