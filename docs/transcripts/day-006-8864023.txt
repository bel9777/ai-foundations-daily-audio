AI Foundations — Day 6: Learning from Labeled Examples

AI Foundations — Day 6 Learning from Labeled Examples Today’s objective: Understand supervised learning: how a model uses examples with known answers to learn a mapping, how training reduces error, and why success on training data is not enough. Time: 8–12 minutes Prerequisites: Days 3–5: the machine-learning workflow, generalization, and the idea that a dataset is a model’s experience. No math or coding is required. --- The core idea Many useful AI systems learn from solved examples. Give a model thousands of loan applications labeled “repaid” or “defaulted,” plant photos labeled “healthy” or “diseased,” or emails labeled “spam” or “not spam.” The model searches for patterns connecting each input to its known answer. It can then predict answers for new inputs. This is supervised learning . “Supervised” does not mean a person watches every calculation. It means the training examples include the answer the model is supposed to learn. A supervised-learning dataset therefore contains pairs: input → target Examples: - photo of a leaf → “healthy” - house details → sale price - customer history → probability of cancellation - email text → “spam” The input supplies evidence. The target supplies the desired answer. --- Concept 1: Labels turn examples into feedback A label is the known answer attached to a training example. A target is the value the model is trained to predict. The words are often used interchangeably, though “label” is especially common for categories. There are two major supervised tasks: 1. Classification predicts a category: healthy/diseased, fraud/not fraud, or one of several animal species. 2. Regression predicts a number: tomorrow’s temperature, a delivery time, or a house price. The distinction matters because the output and the measure of error differ. A classifier might output probabilities such as: - healthy: 0.80 - nutrient deficiency: 0.15 - fungal disease: 0.05 A regression model might output one number, such as 42 minutes. Labels are not automatically true. They may be inconsistent, subjective, delayed, or wrong. If two workers label the same borderline leaf differently, the model receives conflicting feedback. This is label noise : error or inconsistency in the targets. A large dataset with unreliable labels can teach the wrong lesson very efficiently. --- Concept 2: The model learns a mapping, not a rulebook A model is a parameterized function: it transforms an input into a prediction, and its adjustable internal values are called parameters . You can think of a simple model as a machine with many adjustable knobs. One knob may increase the influence of leaf color; another may reduce the influence of shape; thousands or billions of others may represent more subtle patterns. During training, an algorithm adjusts these parameters. The model is not normally handed an explicit rule such as: > If more than 20% of the leaf is yellow, predict nutrient deficiency. Instead, it infers a mapping from examples. That makes supervised learning flexible, but it also makes the result dependent on the examples provided. If every diseased-leaf photo happens to have a blue tray in the background, the model may learn “blue tray means disease.” That correlation works in training but fails in the real world. This is why Day 5’s ideas—representative data, leakage, and bias—directly control what the model can learn. --- Concept 3: Loss converts mistakes into a training signal To improve a model, training needs a way to measure how wrong each prediction is. A loss function turns the difference between a prediction and the target into a number. Lower loss means a better fit according to that measurement. Suppose the correct label is “diseased.” - Model A predicts 90% diseased. - Model B predicts 51% diseased. - Model C predicts 5% diseased. All three produce a prediction, but C should receive the strongest correction. A suitable loss function captures that difference. For regression, a simple loss can be based on the distance between the predicted and actual number. If the actual delivery time is 40 minutes, a prediction of 42 should be penalized less than a prediction of 90. Loss is used during training. A business metric is used to judge usefulness. They are related but not identical. A model may train using a mathematically convenient loss while the business cares about missed diseases, false alarms, dollars saved, or time reduced. --- Concept 4: Training repeats a predict–compare–adjust loop The training loop is: 1. Predict: Run a batch of inputs through the model. 2. Compare: Use the loss function to compare predictions with known targets. 3. Adjust: Change parameters in a direction expected to reduce loss. 4. Repeat: Continue across the training data. An optimizer is the algorithm that decides how to adjust parameters. The most important mental model is not its mathematics; it is that the optimizer uses error as a directional signal. One complete pass through the training dataset is an epoch . Training may run for many epochs. Early on, both training and validation performance may improve. Later, training performance may continue improving while validation performance stalls or worsens. That is the overfitting pattern from Day 4. Training loss answers: “How well are we fitting examples we have seen?” Validation results answer: “Are we learning a pattern likely to work on unseen examples?” You need both. --- Analogy: A student with an answer key Imagine a student studying 1,000 plant-disease flash cards. Each card has a leaf photo on the front and the diagnosis on the back. For each card, the student: - guesses the diagnosis, - checks the answer, - notices the mistake, - adjusts what features they pay attention to. That is supervised training. But suppose every fungal-disease card was photographed on the same wooden table. The student may unconsciously use the table rather than the leaf. They score perfectly on familiar cards and fail when tested with new backgrounds. The answer key provides supervision. It does not guarantee that the student learned the intended concept. A separate test with genuinely new examples reveals whether learning generalized. --- Concrete example: Sorting apples A farm wants to classify apples as sellable or damaged from photos. The team collects 10,000 photos and has trained staff label each image. Each pair looks like: apple image → sellable/damaged The model predicts a probability of damage. During training, its loss is high when it confidently disagrees with the staff label. The optimizer adjusts parameters over repeated epochs. Before deployment, the team tests on apples from different harvest days, cameras, lighting conditions, varieties, and workers. Why? A random split of near-duplicate photos could make validation look excellent while hiding failure on a new season or camera. The team also chooses a decision threshold. Perhaps an apple is rejected when predicted damage probability exceeds 70%. Lowering the threshold catches more damaged apples but also rejects more good ones. The model supplies probabilities; the operation chooses a tradeoff. This separates three decisions: - What examples and labels define the task? - How does training reduce predictive error? - How should the resulting predictions be used? --- Free hands-on exercise: Be the training algorithm Use paper, a note app, or a spreadsheet. No account or code is needed. You are building a simple rule to predict whether a fictional plant needs water. Training examples: Soil feels dry Leaves droop Hot day Needs water --- --- --- --- Yes Yes Yes Yes Yes No Yes Yes No Yes No No No No Yes No Yes Yes No Yes No No No No 1. Start with this rule: Predict Yes only when leaves droop. 2. Compare it with all six labels. Count the mistakes. 3. Adjust the rule: Predict Yes when soil feels dry. 4. Count mistakes again. 5. Test your better rule on this unseen example: dry soil = No, drooping = Yes, hot day = Yes. You should find that the first rule makes two mistakes, while “soil feels dry” fits all six training examples. On the unseen example, it predicts No. Now ask the important question: should you trust it? Not completely. Six invented examples are too few and may omit important cases, such as recently transplanted plants that droop despite wet soil. Low training error is evidence of fit, not proof of generalization. --- What to remember - Supervised learning learns from input–target pairs. - Classification predicts categories; regression predicts numbers. - Loss quantifies prediction error so an optimizer can adjust parameters. - Repeated training can improve fit and eventually cause overfitting. - Labels and data design determine what feedback the model receives. --- Knowledge check 1. What makes a learning problem “supervised”? 2. What is the difference between a loss function and a business metric? 3. Training loss keeps falling, but validation performance gets worse. What is the likely problem? --- Spaced review From Day 3 , recall the workflow: define the task before collecting data or choosing a model. From Day 4 , recall that generalization means performing well on unseen cases, not merely remembering training examples. From Day 5 , recall that a dataset is the model’s experience. In supervised learning, labels are the feedback attached to that experience. Try saying the full chain aloud: task → labeled examples → model predictions → loss → parameter updates → validation → deployment decision --- Answers 1. The training examples include known target answers that provide feedback. 2. A loss function is the numerical training signal used to adjust the model; a business metric measures the outcome people actually care about. 3. Overfitting: the model is fitting the training examples more closely while generalizing less well. --- Tomorrow: Inside a simple neural network We’ll move from the training loop to the model itself: neurons, layers, activations, weights, and how a neural network builds complex patterns from simple calculations. — AI Foundations
