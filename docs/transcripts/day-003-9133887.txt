AI Foundations Day 3: The Basic Machine Learning Workflow Hi Brian, This is your catch-up lesson for Course Day 3 . Course day: 3 Course date: June 25, 2026 Estimated time: 8-12 minutes Objective By the end of this lesson, you should understand the basic workflow most machine learning projects follow: define the task, collect examples, train a model, evaluate it, improve it, and decide whether it is good enough to use. This gives you the practical map behind many AI projects. Later, when we discuss neural networks, transformers, generative AI, retrieval, agents, deployment, and safety, this workflow will still be underneath the surface. Prerequisites You only need two ideas from Days 1 and 2: - AI is software that performs tasks associated with intelligence. - Machine learning is a way to build AI by learning patterns from examples. No math or coding is required today. Concept 1: Start With the Task A machine learning project should begin with a clear task. A task is the specific job you want the model to perform. Examples: - Predict whether an email is spam. - Estimate the sale price of a house. - Identify whether a photo contains a ripe tomato. - Classify a customer message as billing, technical support, refund, or general question. - Predict whether a farm stand item is likely to sell out today. A vague goal like “use AI to improve the business” is not enough. Machine learning needs a more concrete target. A better goal is: “Given product, price, weather, day of week, and recent sales, predict whether this item will sell out today.” The task defines what data you need, what output the model should produce, and how you will judge whether it worked. Concept 2: Collect and Prepare Examples Once the task is clear, you need examples. In supervised learning, each example usually contains: - Inputs: the information the model gets to look at. - Label: the correct answer you want the model to learn. For example, if the task is predicting whether an item will sell out today, one training example might look like this: - Product: ground beef - Price: $8.99/lb - Day: Saturday - Weather: sunny - Inventory at open: 18 packages - Sold out by close: yes The inputs are product, price, day, weather, and inventory. The label is whether it sold out. Data preparation often includes cleaning mistakes, removing duplicates, fixing missing values, and deciding which information should be available to the model. A key beginner idea: better data often matters more than a fancier model. Concept 3: Train the Model Training is the process where the model learns patterns from examples. At first, the model makes weak guesses. During training, it compares its guesses to the correct labels and adjusts itself to do better. A simple way to think about training: 1. The model makes a prediction. 2. The prediction is compared with the correct answer. 3. The model gets a signal about how wrong it was. 4. The model adjusts. 5. This repeats many times. This does not mean the model understands the world the way a person does. It means the model is adjusting internal patterns so its outputs better match the examples. Concept 4: Evaluate the Model Evaluation means testing how well the model performs. Evaluation should use examples the model did not train on. Otherwise, you may only learn that the model remembers its practice questions. For example, suppose a model predicts whether emails are spam. If it saw all the test emails during training, a high score is not very meaningful. You want to know whether it works on new emails. Evaluation asks practical questions: - How often is the model right? - What kinds of mistakes does it make? - Are some mistakes more costly than others? - Does it work across different types of examples? - Is it good enough for the intended use? “Good enough” depends on the task. A movie recommendation can be imperfect and still useful. A medical diagnosis system, loan decision tool, or fraud system has a much higher bar. Concept 5: Improve, Deploy, and Monitor Most AI projects are iterative. Iterative means you improve through repeated cycles instead of expecting the first version to be final. If evaluation shows weak performance, builders may improve the system by: - Adding better examples. - Fixing mislabeled data. - Choosing better inputs. - Trying a different model. - Adjusting training settings. - Narrowing the task. If the model is good enough, it may be deployed . Deployment means putting the model into real use, such as inside an app, workflow, website, report, or automated process. After deployment, the work is not over. Real-world conditions change. Customer behavior changes. Product mixes change. Spam tactics change. Language changes. Weather, markets, and user expectations change. That is why AI systems need monitoring : regularly checking whether they still perform well. The Basic Workflow Here is the full beginner version: 1. Define the task. 2. Collect examples. 3. Prepare the data. 4. Train the model. 5. Evaluate the model on examples it did not train on. 6. Improve the model or data. 7. Deploy if it is good enough. 8. Monitor performance over time. This workflow is not only for simple models. The same broad shape applies to advanced AI systems too, even when the details become more complex. Analogy: Training a New Farm Stand Employee Imagine training a new employee to decide which products need to be restocked first. You would not begin by saying, “Use intelligence.” You would define the task: watch inventory and decide what needs restocking. Then you would provide examples: - When eggs are down to two dozen by 10 AM on Saturday, restock quickly. - When a slow-moving cut has plenty left near closing, do not restock yet. - When weather is bad, expect lower walk-up traffic. - When there is a market day or holiday, expect higher demand. The employee practices, gets feedback, improves, and eventually handles the task independently. But you would still check their decisions, especially when conditions change. Machine learning follows a similar pattern: examples, practice, feedback, evaluation, real-world use, and ongoing monitoring. Concrete Example: Email Triage Suppose you want an AI system to classify incoming emails into four categories: - Needs reply today - Can wait - Newsletter or FYI - Spam or junk The workflow might look like this: Task: Classify each incoming email into one of four categories. Examples: Past emails with the category you would have assigned. Inputs: Sender, subject, message text, whether it includes a question, whether the sender is known, and whether the email mentions deadlines. Labels: The correct category for each past email. Training: The model learns patterns from labeled examples. Evaluation: Test it on emails it did not train on. Improvement: Fix examples where the label was unclear, add more examples of urgent emails, or adjust the categories. Deployment: Use it to suggest email priority. Monitoring: Periodically check whether it is misclassifying important messages. This example also shows why human judgment still matters. If the model marks an important client email as low priority, the mistake could matter. The right use might be “suggest a category” rather than “automatically hide emails.” New Terms Task: The specific job a model is supposed to perform. Input: Information given to the model, such as text, numbers, images, or other features. Label: The correct answer attached to a training example. Training: The process where a model learns patterns from examples. Evaluation: Measuring how well a model performs, especially on examples it did not train on. Iterative: Improved through repeated cycles. Deployment: Putting a model into real use. Monitoring: Checking a deployed model over time to make sure it still works well. Free Hands-On Exercise No tools required. Use paper, a notes app, or a spreadsheet. Pick one task from your life or work. Use this template: Task: What should the model predict or classify? Inputs: What information would the model look at? Label: What is the correct answer in past examples? Evaluation: How would you know whether the model is doing well? Risk: What would be the cost of a bad prediction? Example: Task: Predict whether a farm product will sell out today. Inputs: Product, price, day of week, weather, starting inventory, recent sales. Label: Sold out by close: yes or no. Evaluation: Test on past days the model did not train on. Risk: Overstocking wastes effort; understocking misses sales. Now write your own. Keep it simple. The goal is to practice thinking like an AI project designer. Knowledge Check 1. Why should an AI project start with a clear task? 2. What is the difference between training and evaluation? 3. Why does a deployed model need monitoring? --- Answers 1. A clear task defines what the model should do, what data is needed, what output is expected, and how success will be measured. 2. Training is when the model learns from examples. Evaluation is when we test how well it performs, especially on examples it did not train on. 3. A deployed model needs monitoring because real-world conditions change, and a model that worked before may become less accurate or less useful over time.
