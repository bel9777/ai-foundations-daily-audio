AI Foundations — Day 22: Training Deep Networks Reliably

Day 22 — Training Deep Networks Reliably Objective: Learn the practical habits that make deep neural networks actually improve during training, instead of diverging, getting stuck, or learning very slowly. Prerequisites: You should be comfortable with basic neural network parts, layers and activations, and the idea of backpropagation from Day 21. It also helps to remember that Day 18 covered probability and calibration, which we will revisit when we talk about checking whether a model is truly improving. By now you know how a neural network computes predictions and how backpropagation assigns credit for error. Today we turn that into a training routine you can trust. The main idea is simple: reliable training is not one trick, but a set of small choices that keep learning stable. 1) Start with the loss, not with the architecture Loss is a number that measures how wrong the model is on its training data. Training means changing the network’s weights to make that number smaller. If the loss goes down smoothly, training is probably working. If it jumps around wildly, the model may be learning too fast or too unstably. A useful first habit is to look at the training loss after every few updates. This is the simplest health check in deep learning. If the loss becomes NaN (short for “not a number”), or it explodes upward, the training process is unstable. 2) Learn rate: the step size that controls everything The learning rate is the size of each step when the model updates its weights. You can think of training as walking downhill in fog. The learning rate is how big each step is. If the learning rate is too large, you may overshoot the bottom and bounce around or diverge. If it is too small, training may be painfully slow or appear stuck. This is the most common reason beginners struggle. A model that “doesn’t learn” is often a model with a badly chosen learning rate, not a bad idea overall. Analogy: Imagine trying to find the lowest point in a dark room by feeling the floor with your feet. Tiny steps make you slow but careful. Huge steps make you miss the low spot and stumble around. The learning rate is your step size. 3) Gradients can be too small or too large A gradient is the direction and strength of the change suggested by backpropagation. It tells each weight how to move to reduce the loss. In deep networks, gradients can sometimes become: Vanishing gradients : the gradients become extremely small, so early layers learn very slowly. Exploding gradients : the gradients become very large, so updates become unstable. These problems happen because many layers multiply signals together as information moves forward and backward. Small repeated multiplications can shrink a signal; large repeated multiplications can blow it up. One practical tool is gradient clipping , which means setting a maximum size for gradients before the update happens. Clipping does not solve every problem, but it can prevent rare huge updates from ruining training. 4) Good initialization gives the network a fair start Initialization means the starting values of the network’s weights. If weights start too large, signals can explode. If they start too small, signals can disappear. So we usually choose initial weights carefully rather than randomly in a naive way. The goal is to keep activations and gradients in a healthy range at the start of training. This connects directly to Day 20 on layers and activations: different activations behave differently, so good initialization helps the network use them well. 5) Normalize inputs to make learning easier Normalization means rescaling data so the numbers are more regular, often by shifting them to have a similar center and scale. For example, if one feature ranges from 0 to 1,000 and another ranges from 0 to 1, the model may have trouble learning smoothly unless the inputs are scaled. When inputs have similar scale, gradient-based learning becomes easier because no single feature dominates just because of its units. Think of normalization as putting different instruments in the same tuning system before a performance. Without it, the model hears a messy mix of loud and quiet signals. Real-world example: detecting fraudulent card transactions Suppose you are training a neural network to detect fraudulent credit card transactions. The input features might include transaction amount, time of day, distance from the user’s home, and device type. If transaction amount is measured in dollars from 0 to 5,000, but another feature is just 0 or 1, the large-scale feature may overwhelm learning unless you normalize. If the learning rate is too high, the model may look promising for a few batches and then become unstable. If gradients explode, the model might suddenly start producing nonsense predictions. If gradients vanish, early layers may stop adapting, and the network will fail to capture subtle patterns. Reliable training is what lets the model steadily improve rather than behaving like a lucky guesser. Spaced review: two ideas from earlier lessons From Day 21, backpropagation: backprop is the method that computes how much each weight contributed to the error. Today’s lesson uses that same mechanism, but focuses on making the resulting updates stable enough to trust. From Day 18, calibration and confidence: a model that trains smoothly is not automatically a model that is well calibrated. Calibration means predicted probabilities should match reality. During training, we care about loss and stability; after training, we still need to ask whether the confidence values make sense. From Day 16, linear models and their limits: deep networks can learn more complex patterns than linear models, but they are also harder to train reliably. That extra power comes with extra care requirements. A simple training checklist Watch the training loss. Try a smaller or larger learning rate if the loss behaves badly. Normalize input features. Use sensible weight initialization. Consider gradient clipping if training becomes unstable. You do not need to master every advanced technique at once. In practice, these basics solve many training problems. Free hands-on exercise Use any free notebook environment or even pencil and paper for a small experiment. Take a tiny dataset with just two numeric features. If you do not have one, make up ten data points on paper. Imagine one feature ranges from 1 to 1,000 and the other ranges from 0 to 1. Write down what might happen if you train without normalization. Now rescale the large feature to a 0 to 1 range and predict how training would change. Repeat the thought experiment with two learning rates: one very large and one very small. The goal is not to get a perfect numeric answer. The goal is to build intuition for why stable training depends on scale and step size. Knowledge check What does the learning rate control during training? What is a vanishing gradient?
