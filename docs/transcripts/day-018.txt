AI Foundations — Day 18: Probability, Calibration, and Confidence

Day 18: Probability, Calibration, and Confidence Objective: Learn how to turn model outputs into useful probabilities, understand when “confidence” is trustworthy, and avoid common mistakes when interpreting prediction scores. Prerequisites: You should be comfortable with basic classification ideas, confusion matrices, evaluation metrics like precision and recall, cross-validation, baselines, and the fact that different models can make different tradeoffs. From yesterday, remember that decision trees and ensembles can improve performance, but “better accuracy” does not automatically mean “better probability estimates.” Why this matters Many AI systems do not just make a yes/no prediction. They also produce a score like 0.83 or 12% . People often read that score as “the model is 83% sure.” Sometimes that is roughly true. Sometimes it is badly wrong. Today we will build the missing bridge between prediction and probability , so you can tell when a score is useful and when it is misleading. 1) Probability: a way to represent uncertainty Probability is a number between 0 and 1 that represents how likely something is to happen. A probability of 0 means impossible, 1 means certain, and 0.5 means a coin-flip level of uncertainty. In machine learning, a probability should answer a practical question: “Out of many cases that look like this, how often is the answer positive?” Example: if a medical model says a patient has a 0.20 probability of a condition, then among many similar patients, about 20 out of 100 would actually have it. 2) Confidence scores are not always probabilities A confidence score is any number a model gives to show how strongly it favors one answer over another. That score may be a true probability, but it may also just be a ranking score. This is a very important distinction: Probability is meant to be interpretable as frequency. Confidence score may only mean “more certain than other options.” For example, one model may output 0.90 because it is really calibrated as a probability. Another may output 0.90 because it produces a high internal score after normalization. The numbers look the same, but their meaning may differ. 3) Calibration: when predicted probabilities match reality Calibration means the model’s predicted probabilities match the real-world frequency of outcomes. If a model is well calibrated, then among all examples where it predicts 0.80 , about 80% should actually be positive. If a model predicts 0.80 for 100 cases, and only 40 of them are truly positive, the model is overconfident . It says “80%” but the real answer is closer to 40%. If a model predicts 0.30 but 60% of those cases are positive, it is underconfident . It is too cautious. Memorable analogy: the weather forecaster Think of probability like a weather forecast. If a forecaster says “30% chance of rain,” they are not saying rain is unlikely in a vague emotional sense. They are saying that on days with similar conditions, it rains about 3 times out of 10. A good forecaster is not the one who always sounds confident. A good forecaster is the one whose percentages match reality over time. That is calibration. 4) Confidence is useful, but it needs context A model can be confident for the wrong reasons. This is why confidence should be used with care. Two models can have the same accuracy but very different calibration. One might be better for decisions that require probability, such as: setting a medical screening threshold, deciding whether to review a transaction manually, choosing how much risk to take. If you only care about ranking cases from most likely to least likely, a raw score may be enough. If you need to compare risk against cost, you want probabilities that are as calibrated as possible. 5) A concrete real-world example: loan approvals Suppose a bank uses a model to estimate whether a loan applicant will repay a loan. Applicant A gets a predicted probability of 0.90 . Applicant B gets 0.60 . Applicant C gets 0.20 . If the model is calibrated, the bank can use those numbers in a decision rule. For example, it may approve applicants above 0.75 , review applicants between 0.40 and 0.75 , and reject the rest. That is much more useful than just asking whether the model is “right” or “wrong” on a test set. But if the model is overconfident, its 0.90 estimates may actually behave like 0.70 . Then the bank may take on more risk than it intended. Review: connect this to earlier lessons From Day 12 , we learned that evaluation metrics should match the goal. Probability is another example of that idea: if your goal is risk management, a nicely calibrated probability is often more useful than raw accuracy. From Day 13 , we learned about classification tradeoffs. A threshold turns probabilities into predictions. Lowering the threshold increases positives; raising it decreases positives. Calibration helps you choose that threshold more intelligently. From Day 14 , cross-validation gave us more reliable comparisons. The same is true here: if you estimate calibration on a single split, you can get a misleading picture. Use careful validation when comparing models. From Day 15 , a baseline is still valuable. Even a simple baseline can be surprisingly well or poorly calibrated, and that gives you a reference point. From Days 16 and 17 , we saw that linear models and trees/ensembles can behave differently. Some models tend to produce better probability estimates than others, but no model should be trusted without checking calibration. How to think about calibration in practice There are three simple questions to ask: Does the score rank cases well? This is about ordering from high risk to low risk. Does the score match reality? This is calibration. What decision will use the score? If the answer changes with cost, threshold choice matters. A model can do well on ranking but poorly on calibration. That means it can identify which cases are more risky without giving trustworthy percentages. Free hands-on exercise You do not need paid tools for this. Use paper, a spreadsheet, or a simple text file. Exercise: test calibration with 10 guesses Think of 10 yes/no events you can predict from your own life or from a simple scenario. Examples: “Will it rain tomorrow?”, “Will I go for a walk?”, or “Will this email require a follow-up?” For each event, write down a probability such as 0.20 , 0.50 , or 0.80 . Later, record what actually happened. Group your predictions into rough bins, such as 0.0-0.3 , 0.4-0.6 , and 0.7-1.0 . For each bin, count how often the event really happened. What to look for: if your 0.7-1.0 guesses come true only half the time, you are overconfident. If your 0.0-0.3 guesses come true far more often than expected, you are underconfident. This simple exercise gives you the intuition behind calibration without any special software. Three common mistakes Mistake 1: treating every score as a probability. A score is only a probability if it behaves like one. Mistake 2: confusing accuracy with calibration. A model can be accurate overall and still give poor probabilities. Mistake 3: trusting confidence without checking the data. Confidence should be measured, not assumed. Knowledge check 1. What does it mean for a model to be well calibrated? 2. Why is a confidence score not always the same thing as a probability? 3. If a model predicts 0.80 for many cases, but only 40% of those cases are actually positive, is the model overconfident or underconfident? ------------------------------ Answers 1. A model is well calibrated when its predicted probabilities match real-world frequencies. For example, cases predicted at 0.80 should be positive about 80% of the time. 2. A confidence score may only be a ranking or internal score. It tells you how strongly the model favors an answer, but it does not necessarily mean the number can be read as a true probability. 3. The model is overconfident , because it predicts 80% but the real frequency is only 40%.
