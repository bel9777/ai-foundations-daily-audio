AI Foundations — Day 31: Human Feedback and Preference Optimization

Day 31 — Human Feedback and Preference Optimization Objective: Understand how human feedback helps AI systems become more useful, safer, and better aligned with what people want, and learn the basic idea behind preference optimization. Prerequisites: You should be comfortable with the ideas from Days 26–30: transformers, tokens and context windows, pretraining, next-token prediction, and fine-tuning for instruction following. Today builds directly on those ideas. Up to now, you have seen that a language model can learn patterns from huge amounts of text, predict the next token, and then be fine-tuned to follow instructions. But “follows instructions” does not automatically mean “does what people prefer.” Human feedback and preference optimization are the steps that help close that gap. 1) Why human feedback matters Human feedback means people give the model examples of what they consider better or worse answers. This can be as simple as choosing between two responses and saying which one is more helpful, clearer, safer, or more polite. Why is this needed? Because text on the internet contains many patterns that are useful, but also some that are confusing, misleading, or rude. Pretraining teaches the model to imitate patterns in text. Fine-tuning teaches it to respond to instructions. Human feedback helps teach it preferences —what humans tend to want when several answers are possible. Preference means a choice for one option over another based on what seems better for a goal. In AI, the goal is often something like “be helpful,” “be honest,” or “be safe.” 2) The basic workflow: compare, score, improve A common preference-optimization workflow has three parts: Generate candidates: The model produces more than one possible answer to the same prompt. Collect feedback: A person compares the answers and picks the better one, or ranks several answers from best to worst. Update the model: The training process uses those comparisons to make future answers more likely to match human preferences. Preference optimization means adjusting a model so that it tends to produce outputs people prefer. It does not mean the model “knows” what is best in a human sense; it means the model is trained to match human judgments more often. There are many technical ways to do this, but the core idea is simple: if one answer is repeatedly preferred over another, the model should learn from that pattern. 3) Reward signals and reward models Some systems use a reward signal , which is a number that tells the model how good an answer was according to a target. Higher reward means better. Think of it like a score in a game. A reward model is a separate model trained to predict which answers humans would prefer. Instead of asking humans to judge every possible output, the system learns a rough stand-in for human preferences. Then the main model can be guided by that learned scoring system. This is useful, but it has limits. A reward model is only an estimate of human judgment, so it can be wrong. That is why human review still matters. 4) A memorable analogy: a writing coach and a judge Imagine a student writer learning from a teacher. Pretraining is like reading thousands of books and essays to absorb language patterns. Fine-tuning is like practicing essay prompts with a tutor who says, “Answer the question directly.” Human feedback is like a judge comparing two drafts and saying which one is clearer, more accurate, or more respectful. Preference optimization is like the student gradually learning to write more like the drafts the judge keeps choosing. The key point: the judge is not just checking grammar. The judge is shaping style, usefulness, and behavior. 5) A real-world example Suppose you ask an AI: “Explain why leaves change color in autumn.” The model might produce two answers: Answer A: short, correct, but vague Answer B: clear, correct, and includes a simple explanation about chlorophyll A human reviewer will likely prefer Answer B because it is more helpful. If this happens across many examples, the model learns that clear explanations are preferred when the user asks for a concept explanation. This is especially important in customer support, tutoring, and medical or legal information settings, where clarity and caution matter a lot. 6) How this connects to instruction following and earlier lessons Day 30 introduced instruction following , which means answering the user’s request instead of just continuing text. Preference optimization goes a step further: among many possible instruction-following answers, it helps select the one humans tend to like best. Day 27 covered tokens and context windows . Those still matter today because feedback is based on the model’s actual output, which is generated token by token within a limited context window. If the model cannot “see” part of the prompt or previous conversation, it may produce an answer that humans would rank lower. Day 29 covered next-token prediction . That is still the engine underneath everything: the model always predicts the next token. Human feedback does not replace that engine; it reshapes which next-token patterns become more likely in practice. 7) Important limits and cautions Human feedback is helpful, but it is not magic. A few important limits: People disagree: Different reviewers may prefer different styles or levels of detail. Bias can enter: If the feedback comes from a narrow group of people, the model may learn their preferences too strongly. Preferences are not truth: A more pleasing answer is not always a more correct answer. Over-optimizing can be risky: If the system focuses too much on pleasing responses, it may become overly cautious, vague, or flattering. So the goal is not simply “make the model agree with humans.” The goal is to make it useful while keeping accuracy, honesty, and safety in view. Free hands-on exercise You do not need paid tools for this exercise. Use a notebook, a text editor, or paper. Write one prompt you might ask an AI, such as: Explain photosynthesis to a 10-year-old. Imagine or generate three different answers yourself. Rank them from best to worst using three criteria: clarity , correctness , and completeness . Write one sentence explaining why the best answer won. Now rewrite the weakest answer so it would likely rank higher. As you do this, notice that “best” is not just “most detailed.” Often the best answer is the one that is easiest to understand and least likely to mislead. Knowledge check What is the difference between human feedback and pretraining ? What does preference optimization try to make the model do? Why is a reward model useful, and why is it not perfect? ------------------------------ Answers Pretraining teaches the model from massive text patterns in general, while human feedback uses people’s judgments to shape which outputs are preferred. It tries to make the model more likely to produce answers humans judge as better, such as clearer, more helpful, or safer answers. A reward model is useful because it can approximate human preferences and reduce the need for constant human rating, but it is not perfect because it can misjudge answers or reflect biased feedback.
