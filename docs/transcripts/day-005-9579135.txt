AI Foundations — Day 5: Data Is the Model's Experience

AI Foundations Day 5: Data Is the Model’s Experience Estimated time: 8–12 minutes Objective By the end of this lesson, you will be able to explain why an AI system’s behavior depends on its data, identify features and targets in a dataset, and spot three common data risks: poor representation, bias, and leakage. Prerequisites You only need the ideas from the earlier lessons: - AI systems learn patterns from examples rather than receiving every rule by hand. - A basic machine-learning workflow separates training from evaluation. - Good evaluation asks whether a model can generalize to new cases. The central idea A model does not experience the world directly. It experiences a dataset: a selected, measured, encoded slice of the world. That distinction matters. If an AI system has never seen an important situation in its data, it cannot reliably learn how to handle it. If the data describes the world inaccurately, the model may learn the inaccuracy. If the data accidentally reveals the answer, evaluation can look excellent even though the model has learned a shortcut. Think of data as the model’s experience. Model architecture matters, but data determines what evidence the model gets to learn from. Concept 1: Dataset, example, feature, and target A dataset is an organized collection of examples used to train, evaluate, or operate an AI system. An example is one individual case. In a system that predicts whether a farm delivery will arrive late, one completed delivery could be one example. A feature is information supplied to the model as input. Possible delivery features include distance, weather, departure time, vehicle type, and the number of scheduled stops. A target —also called a label —is the answer the model is meant to predict. Here, the target could be “late” or “on time,” or it could be the number of minutes late. The target turns a collection of records into a supervised learning problem. During training, the model compares predictions with known targets and adjusts itself to reduce error. A useful question is: At prediction time, what information will genuinely be available? A feature should usually pass that test. If it exists only after the outcome occurs, it may create leakage, which we will cover shortly. Concept 2: Representation Representation is the form in which real-world information is encoded for a model. A model does not receive “a rainy morning” as lived experience. It might receive: - precipitation: 0.4 inches - temperature: 48°F - hour: 7 - road condition: wet Text models receive text broken into units called tokens . Image models receive numerical pixel values or learned visual features. Tabular models receive columns. The chosen representation determines which distinctions are visible to the model. Analogy: teaching from a photo album Imagine teaching someone about farm operations using only a photo album. Their understanding depends on which photos you include, what the photos show, and what captions you attach. If the album contains only sunny harvest days, the learner may conclude that farm work always happens in good weather. If every photo of a successful crop includes a red tractor, they may incorrectly decide that red tractors cause good yields. If captions reveal information that would not be available during a real decision, the learner can appear knowledgeable while relying on clues they will not have later. A dataset is that photo album. Features are what can be seen or read in each entry. Targets are the captions containing the answers. Concept 3: Data quality and representativeness Data quality asks whether the records are accurate, complete, consistent, and correctly labeled. Common quality problems include: - missing values - duplicate records - incorrect measurements - inconsistent units - outdated entries - wrong labels Representativeness asks whether the dataset adequately reflects the situations in which the model will be used. These are different. A perfectly accurate dataset of summer deliveries may still be unrepresentative for year-round predictions in western New York. The records can be correct but incomplete as experience. When real operating conditions differ from training conditions, the model faces distribution shift . For example, a delivery model trained before a new route policy, severe fuel shortage, or major customer expansion may encounter patterns unlike those in its training data. A practical test is to compare the dataset with deployment across: - time periods - locations - seasons - customer or user groups - equipment types - rare but costly situations Average performance can hide failure in a small but important slice. Concept 4: Bias in data Data bias is a systematic distortion in what is collected, measured, labeled, or omitted. Bias does not require bad intent. It can arise from ordinary business processes. Suppose a model predicts which maintenance requests are urgent. Historical records may reflect which problems were reported, not every problem that existed. If one team reports issues promptly while another works around them quietly, the dataset may make the first team look less reliable even if it is simply more diligent. Several common sources of bias are: - selection bias: included examples differ systematically from excluded cases - measurement bias: a variable is measured more accurately for some cases than others - label bias: recorded outcomes reflect inconsistent human judgment - historical bias: past decisions or inequalities are embedded in the data The right response is not “remove all bias,” which is rarely realistic. It is to identify relevant distortions, measure their effects, document limits, and improve collection or evaluation where the stakes justify it. Concept 5: Data leakage Data leakage occurs when training or evaluation uses information that would not legitimately be available when a real prediction is made. Leakage is dangerous because it creates misleadingly strong performance. Example Suppose we want to predict whether an invoice will be paid more than 30 days late. The dataset includes: - invoice amount - customer history - invoice date - payment date - number of collection calls “Payment date” directly reveals the answer and is unavailable when the invoice is issued. “Number of collection calls” may also occur after lateness begins. A model using these fields could score extremely well without helping make an early prediction. Leakage can also happen through dataset splitting. If repeated records from the same customer appear in both training and test sets, the model may partially memorize customer identity. A grouped split , keeping all records from the same customer in one partition, can give a more honest test. The general rule is: recreate the information boundary that will exist at the real decision moment. Worked example: predicting late produce deliveries Assume the goal is to predict, at departure time, whether a delivery will be more than 20 minutes late. One example: one historical delivery. Possible features available at departure: - planned distance - scheduled departure time - actual departure delay - forecast weather - number of stops - vehicle category - recent average route duration Target: whether arrival exceeded the promised time by more than 20 minutes. Now audit the data: 1. Quality: Are departure and arrival timestamps reliable? Are time zones consistent? 2. Representation: Is weather encoded for the correct location and time? 3. Representativeness: Does the dataset cover winter, holidays, new routes, and unusually large orders? 4. Bias: Are canceled deliveries absent, making the dataset look easier than operations really are? 5. Leakage: Does any feature use actual arrival information or notes written after delivery? 6. Evaluation design: Are repeated routes, drivers, or customers divided in a way that prevents memorization from inflating results? Notice that “more data” alone does not answer these questions. Ten million misleading records can produce a confidently misleading model. Free exercise: make a mini data card Choose one prediction that could be useful in your work or daily life. Examples: equipment failure, late delivery, customer reorder, crop yield, or household energy use. Write six short lines: 1. Decision: What decision will the prediction support? 2. Example: What does one row or case represent? 3. Target: What exactly should be predicted, and by when? 4. Features: List three inputs genuinely available at prediction time. 5. Coverage risk: Name one season, group, location, or rare event that may be underrepresented. 6. Leakage risk: Name one tempting field that could reveal the outcome too late. Then add one sentence: “I would not trust this model until I tested .” This compact document is a data card : a record of what the data represents, how it was collected, where it may fail, and what responsible use requires. Knowledge check 1. What is the difference between a feature and a target? 2. How can a dataset be high quality but still unrepresentative? 3. Why can data leakage make a weak model appear strong? Answers 1. A feature is an input available to the model; the target is the outcome the model is trained to predict. 2. Its records may be accurate and complete for the cases collected while omitting important deployment conditions, such as winter operations, new customers, rare failures, or specific locations. 3. Leakage supplies information that would not be available at the real prediction moment, allowing the model to exploit an answer-revealing shortcut and inflate evaluation results. Spaced review From earlier lessons, retrieve these answers before reading the prompts below: - What does it mean for a model to generalize ? - Why do we keep evaluation data separate from training data? - Where does training fit in the machine-learning workflow? Review: - Generalization means performing well on relevant new examples, not merely remembering training cases. - Separate evaluation data tests whether learned patterns transfer to unseen cases. - Training comes after defining the task and preparing data; it is followed by evaluation, improvement, deployment, and monitoring. Connect today’s lesson to those ideas: an honest test split is necessary, but it is not sufficient. If training and test data share the same blind spots or leakage, both can produce false confidence. Takeaway A model learns from the evidence it is given—not from reality in full. Strong AI work therefore asks: - What does one example represent? - Which inputs are truly available? - Does the data cover deployment conditions? - What systematic distortions exist? - Could anything reveal the answer improperly? Treat the dataset as the model’s experience, and evaluate whether that experience is adequate for the job.
