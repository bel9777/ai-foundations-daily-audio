AI Foundations — Day 23: Embeddings: Meaning as Geometry

Day 23 — Embeddings: Meaning as Geometry Objective: By the end of today, you will understand what an embedding is, why it helps machines work with language and other messy data, and how “meaning” can be represented as positions in a geometric space. Prerequisites: You should already be comfortable with basic machine learning ideas, especially features, neural networks, representations, backpropagation, and the training basics from Days 19–22. Up to now, we have talked a lot about models that take inputs and turn them into predictions. Today we zoom in on one of the most important building blocks inside modern AI systems: the embedding . An embedding is a learned numeric representation of an item, such as a word, product, user, or image patch. The key idea is that similar things end up with similar numbers. In other words, the model learns a kind of geometry of meaning . 1) Why raw identifiers are not enough Suppose you want a model to understand the words cat , dog , and banana . If you encode each word as a simple label like 1 , 2 , and 3 , the numbers themselves imply fake order and distance. The model might incorrectly think dog is “closer” to banana than to cat just because of the labels. This is a reminder from Day 20: neural networks learn better when the representation of the input matches the structure of the problem. A representation is just the way we turn real-world data into numbers a model can use. Embeddings solve this by giving each item a vector instead of a single label. 2) What a vector is, and why geometry matters A vector is a list of numbers, like [0.2, -1.4, 3.0] . You can think of a vector as a point in space. If two vectors are near each other, the model treats the items they represent as more similar. This is where the geometric idea comes in: a model can place related things near one another and unrelated things far apart. For example: cat and dog might land near each other king and queen might land near each other banana and airplane would likely be far apart Important definition: distance means a numerical measure of how far apart two vectors are. A common choice is Euclidean distance, which is the ordinary straight-line distance. 3) How embeddings are learned An embedding is not usually hand-designed. It is learned during training. That means the model starts with random vectors and updates them using the training process we discussed in Days 21 and 22. Here is the basic idea: The model makes a prediction. It gets an error signal from the loss function. Using backpropagation , the model figures out how to adjust the embedding vectors. Over many examples, useful patterns emerge. Recall from Day 21 that backpropagation is the method for assigning credit or blame to each part of a network for the final error. Embedding vectors get that same treatment. And from Day 22, we know training is about doing this reliably over many small updates without the process becoming unstable. 4) A memorable analogy: the giant library map Imagine a giant library with millions of books. If every book were given a random shelf number, the numbers would not tell you anything useful. But suppose someone created a map where books on similar topics are placed near each other: history books in one region, novels in another, math books in another. That map would let you navigate by meaning, not just by ID. An embedding is like that library map. The coordinates are not the meaning themselves, but they organize meaning in a way the model can use. 5) A concrete real-world example: shopping recommendations Online stores often use embeddings for products and customers. A product embedding might place running shoes near athletic socks and water bottles, because shoppers who buy one often buy the others. A customer embedding might reflect shopping habits such as budget, style, or brand preferences. Then the system can compare vectors to make recommendations. If a customer vector is near the vector for a certain product category, the model may recommend items from that area of the space. This does not mean the model “understands” products the way a human does. It means the embeddings capture patterns useful for prediction. 6) Three common uses of embeddings Words and tokens: Each word or piece of a word gets a vector. A token is a chunk of text used by a model, which may be a full word, part of a word, or punctuation. Categories: Things like city names, product IDs, or user IDs can be mapped into vectors. Search and retrieval: Similar questions, documents, or images can be placed near each other so the system can find relevant matches. Embeddings are especially useful when the input space is large and sparse. Sparse means mostly empty, with many possible items and few active ones at a time. For example, one-hot encoding a huge vocabulary creates long vectors with almost all zeros. Embeddings are much more compact and often more useful. 7) What to remember about “meaning as geometry” Here is the core idea in one sentence: embeddings turn abstract things into points in a space where distance and direction capture useful relationships . That is why people say meaning becomes geometry. The model is not storing a dictionary definition. It is arranging items so that their positions help it solve a task. This is also why embeddings are so flexible. A good embedding space can support analogy-like behavior, similarity search, classification, and recommendation—all because geometry gives the model a structure to work with. Spaced review: two ideas from earlier lessons From Day 20, representations: the way we encode data matters. Embeddings are a powerful representation because they let the model learn a useful input space instead of forcing us to hand-design one. From Day 18, calibration and confidence: embeddings help models make better predictions, but a useful internal representation does not automatically mean the model is well calibrated. A model can have strong similarity structure in its embeddings and still be overconfident or underconfident in its final outputs. From Day 22, reliable training: embeddings are learned with the same training machinery as other neural-network weights, so stable optimization matters. If training is noisy or poorly tuned, the embedding space may be less useful. Hands-on exercise: build a tiny embedding intuition test No paid tools are needed. Use pen and paper, a spreadsheet, or a plain text editor. Pick 8 items from a domain you know well. Example: 8 foods, 8 animals, or 8 movies. Give each item two numbers by hand so that similar items are placed near each other. For example, for foods you might put fruits near one area and meals near another. Draw the points on a simple 2D grid. Now ask: which items are close together? Which are far apart? Does the layout match your intuition? Finally, imagine one new item and place it where it belongs. That is the basic job an embedding space tries to do. If you want a slightly more advanced version, use a spreadsheet and assign coordinates such as (x, y) based on features like sweet/salty and cold/hot, then see whether similar items cluster. Knowledge check 1. What is an embedding in plain language? 2. Why is a vector representation better than a simple numeric label like 1 , 2 , 3 for words? 3. How do backpropagation and training help create useful embeddings? ------------------------------ Answers 1. An embedding is a learned list of numbers that represents an item in a way that places similar items near each other in a space. 2. A simple label suggests fake order and distance, while a vector can capture relationships like similarity and difference through geometry. 3. Backpropagation tells the model how to adjust the embedding vectors to reduce error, and training repeats those updates until the vectors become useful for the task.
