AI Foundations — Day 16: Linear Models and Their Limits

Day 16: Linear Models and Their Limits Objective: By the end of today, you will understand what a linear model is, when it works well, why it is so useful, and where it starts to fail. Prerequisites: You should be comfortable with the ideas from Days 10–15: loss functions, gradient descent, evaluation metrics, confusion matrices, cross-validation, and baselines. If those terms feel familiar, you are ready. Linear models are one of the simplest and most important tools in machine learning. They are often the first model people try because they are easy to train, fast to evaluate, and surprisingly strong on many problems. But they also have clear limits, and understanding those limits will help you choose better models later. 1) What a linear model is A linear model predicts an output by combining input values with weights and adding them together. A weight is just a number that says how important an input is. A simple example is: prediction = w1 feature1 + w2 feature2 + b Here, a feature is an input variable, like house size or number of bedrooms. The b is called the bias or intercept ; it is the model’s starting point before the inputs are added in. If you are predicting house prices, features might include square footage, neighborhood score, and age of the home. A linear model says each feature contributes in a straight-line way. For example, it might learn that every extra square foot adds about the same amount to the predicted price. 2) Why linear models are useful Linear models are popular for three practical reasons: They are fast. Training and using them usually takes less time than more complex models. They are easier to understand. You can often inspect the weights and see which features push predictions up or down. They are a strong baseline. A baseline is the simplest useful model you compare against. If a fancy model cannot beat a linear model, the fancy model may not be worth the extra complexity. This is a good place to connect back to Day 15: baselines are not just placeholders. They are a reality check. Linear models are often the next step up from a very simple baseline, but still simple enough to trust and debug. 3) Linear regression and linear classification There are two common uses of linear models: Linear regression predicts a number, such as price, temperature, or delivery time. Linear classification predicts a category, such as spam or not spam, or yes or no. In classification, the model usually computes a score from the inputs. If the score is above a threshold, it predicts one class; if it is below, it predicts the other. A threshold is a cutoff value used to turn a score into a category. This connects to Day 12 and Day 13: the threshold changes your evaluation metrics and your confusion matrix . For example, lowering the threshold may catch more spam messages, but it may also mark more normal emails as spam. That is the tradeoff between catching positives and making false alarms. 4) The shape of a linear model Imagine a ruler laid across a scatterplot of points. A linear model is like that ruler: it draws the best straight path through the data. This is a memorable analogy because it captures both the strength and the weakness of the method. Analogy: A linear model is like using a straight ruler to estimate the path of a winding road. If the road is mostly straight, the ruler works well. If the road twists and turns, the ruler misses important bends. That is the core idea behind linear models: they are excellent when the world is approximately straight, and less effective when the real pattern is curved, stepped, or highly interactive. 5) Limits: when linear models struggle Linear models have several important limits: They cannot easily capture curvature. If the relationship between inputs and output bends, a straight-line model may underfit. They miss interactions unless you add them. An interaction means one feature matters differently depending on another feature. For example, the effect of engine size on car price may depend on car brand. They can be sensitive to outliers. An outlier is a data point far away from the others. A few unusual points can pull the line in the wrong direction. They assume the chosen features are enough. If important information is missing, even a perfect linear fit on the available features will still be limited. When a linear model performs badly, the problem is not always the model alone. Sometimes the features are weak, the data is noisy, or the target itself is hard to predict. This is where cross-validation from Day 14 helps: if the model is weak across many splits, the issue is probably not just a lucky or unlucky train-test split. Concrete real-world example Suppose you are predicting apartment rent using just square footage. A linear model might do a decent job because bigger apartments usually cost more. But the real world is messier: An apartment near a subway may cost more than a larger one farther away. A luxury building may charge more even for a small unit. Prices may rise quickly after a certain size, then level off. A straight-line model can capture the overall upward trend, but it may miss those bends and special cases. It is useful, but not complete. How linear models connect to loss and optimization From Day 10, you learned that a loss function defines what “wrong” means. Linear models are usually trained by choosing weights that minimize loss. From Day 11, you learned that gradient descent is one common way to search for better weights. This matters because linear models are mathematically friendly. Their simple structure makes training easier to analyze and often easier to optimize. That is one reason they remain useful even in a world with more complex methods. Free hands-on exercise Try this with any free spreadsheet tool or even paper and a calculator: Make a tiny dataset with one feature and one target. For example: Hours studied: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 Test score: 52, 55, 61, 66, 68 Draw a scatterplot or just look at the pairs. Fit your own straight-line rule by eye. For example, estimate how much the score changes per extra hour studied. Use your rule to predict the score at 6 hours and 0 hours. Now ask: where might the line fail? Would it still work if the last two scores jumped sharply, or if the relationship flattened out? Optional extra step: compare your rule to a simple baseline from Day 15, such as predicting the average score for every student. Ask which is better and why. This is a nice reminder that a model should beat a sensible baseline before you trust it. Quick review of earlier ideas Keep two earlier lessons in mind: Loss functions: A linear model is only as good as the loss it is trained to reduce. If the loss does not match the real goal, the model may optimize the wrong thing. Evaluation metrics: A model that lowers loss is not automatically useful. For classification, you still need metrics like accuracy, precision, recall, or their tradeoffs to judge real performance. Knowledge check 1) What does a linear model do with the input features? 2) Why can a linear model be a good baseline? 3) What is one situation where a linear model may fail? ------------------------------ Answers 1) It multiplies each feature by a weight, adds the results, and usually adds a bias term. 2) It is fast, simple, and often strong enough to give a useful comparison point before trying more complex models. 3) It may fail when the true pattern is curved, depends on interactions, or is strongly affected by outliers.
