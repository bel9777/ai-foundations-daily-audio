AI Foundations — Day 41: Grounding Answers in Sources

Why This Matters

AI answers can sound certain even when the evidence is weak. Today you will learn grounding: connecting an answer to specific source material so another person can check it. Grounding does not promise perfection. It creates a visible trail from a claim back to the document, note, policy, or record that supports it. Ask yourself one useful question: if this answer affects a decision, can I show where it came from? That is the discipline you can use today.

The Mental Model

Imagine a museum audio guide. It can explain a painting, but a good guide also points you to the exhibit label. Without the label, you only hear a persuasive story. With it, you can compare the story with the evidence. A grounded AI answer works the same way. It gives an answer, then shows the source that supports it. Retrieval gets evidence into view; grounding lets a reader trace the response back to it. Also separate what the source says from what the model infers. An attributable statement is directly supported by a source. An inference is a conclusion drawn from that source. If a policy states that refunds are allowed within thirty days, that is a source fact. Saying a request on day thirty-one is likely out of scope is an inference. Both may be useful, but they should not be blended together. Faithful summaries follow this same rule. They stay close to the evidence and avoid adding unsupported details. When the sources do not support a claim, the useful answer is simply that the claim is not supported. That boundary prevents a fluent answer from becoming an uncheckable guess. A Real-World Example

Picture a team answering a customer question about a refund policy. The policy says that customers may request a refund within thirty days of purchase and that digital products are not refundable after download. A grounded answer states the conclusion clearly, names the policy section, and includes the important wording. Then it labels the additional conclusion: if the customer downloaded a digital product, the policy does not support a refund for that item. The first part is evidence. The second part is inference. That format helps with uncertainty too. If the policy says nothing about a failed download, the answer should say that the source does not address the case. It should not manufacture an exception. This same pattern works with meeting notes, operating procedures, spreadsheets, and logs. Give the answer, expose the supporting evidence, and mark anything that goes beyond the source as interpretation. The result is easier to audit, discuss, revise, and use responsibly.
