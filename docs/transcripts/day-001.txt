AI Foundations Day 1: What AI Is (and What It Is Not) Hi Brian, Welcome to Day 1 of your AI Foundations course. Today is Course Day 1 , because this course starts on June 23, 2026 . Objective By the end of today’s 8-12 minute lesson, you should be able to explain what AI is, what it is not, and how to think about AI systems without getting buried in technical jargon. Prerequisites None. Today starts from zero. You do not need to know programming, math, statistics, or how neural networks work yet. The only helpful prerequisite is curiosity about how computers can appear to reason, classify, write, recommend, or make decisions. Today’s Big Idea Artificial intelligence is software that performs tasks we normally associate with human judgment, pattern recognition, language, prediction, or decision-making. AI is not magic. It is not a mind in the human sense. It is a collection of methods for getting computers to do useful things when the instructions are too complex to write out by hand. A normal computer program follows explicit instructions: > If this happens, do that. An AI system often works differently: > Learn patterns from examples, then use those patterns on new situations. That shift is the foundation for almost everything else in this course. Concept 1: AI Is A Broad Category Artificial intelligence is the broad field of making computers perform tasks that seem to require intelligence. Examples include: - Recognizing speech - Translating languages - Recommending products - Detecting fraud - Identifying objects in photos - Writing summaries - Answering questions - Planning steps toward a goal AI is the umbrella term. Later, we will study major branches inside it, including machine learning, deep learning, generative AI, retrieval, agents, and evaluation. For now, remember this: AI is the broad goal. Machine learning is one common way to achieve it. Generative AI is one type of AI that creates new content. Concept 2: Rules Versus Learning Older software is usually built from rules written by humans. For example, a simple spam filter might say: - If the subject contains “free money,” mark as spam. - If the sender is on a blocked list, mark as spam. - If the email has five suspicious links, mark as spam. That works until spammers change their wording. A machine learning system takes a different approach. Instead of hand-writing every rule, developers provide many examples of spam and not-spam. The system finds patterns in those examples and uses them to judge new emails. This is why AI can be powerful: it can handle messy situations where the rulebook would be too large or brittle. It is also why AI can be risky: if the examples are incomplete, biased, outdated, or misunderstood, the system can learn the wrong patterns. Concept 3: AI Makes Predictions, Not Guarantees A useful mental model is this: Most AI systems are prediction engines. Prediction does not only mean forecasting the future. It can mean guessing the most likely answer, label, next word, category, action, or pattern. Examples: - In a photo app: “This image probably contains a dog.” - In a sales forecast: “Next month’s demand is likely to be around this level.” - In a chatbot: “Given this conversation, the next helpful response is probably this.” - In a recommendation system: “This customer may be interested in this product.” This matters because AI outputs should often be treated as probabilistic , not automatically true. A calculator gives exact arithmetic. An AI model gives a best guess based on patterns it has learned. Sometimes that guess is excellent. Sometimes it is confidently wrong. Concept 4: Intelligence Is Task-Specific When people say “AI is intelligent,” it is easy to imagine human-like understanding. A better beginner mental model is: AI systems are usually good at specific types of tasks, under specific conditions, with specific limits. A model might be excellent at drafting text but poor at checking whether a business rule is current. Another model might classify medical images well but know nothing about your farm website. A recommendation model might be good at product suggestions but useless for writing emails. So the practical question is not, “Is this AI intelligent?” The better question is: What task is this system designed for, what information does it have, and how will we know whether it worked? That question will keep you grounded throughout the course. New Terms Artificial intelligence (AI): The broad field of building software that performs tasks associated with human intelligence, such as recognizing patterns, using language, making predictions, or choosing actions. Machine learning (ML): A way to build AI systems by learning patterns from examples instead of programming every rule by hand. Model: The learned pattern system that takes an input and produces an output. For example, a spam model takes an email and predicts whether it is spam. Input: The information given to an AI system, such as a question, image, spreadsheet row, customer history, or prompt. Output: The result produced by an AI system, such as an answer, label, recommendation, generated image, or forecast. Prediction: The model’s best estimate of the right answer, category, next word, or likely outcome. Probabilistic: Based on likelihood rather than certainty. A probabilistic system can be useful without being guaranteed correct. Analogy: AI As A New Employee Who Learns From Examples Imagine hiring a new employee to sort incoming emails. You could write a giant manual: - If the email says this, put it here. - If the customer asks that, send it there. - If the message has this tone, escalate it. That manual would quickly become impossible to maintain. Instead, you might show the employee 1,000 past emails and how each one was handled. Over time, they notice patterns. They learn what urgent messages look like, which customers need careful handling, and which messages are routine. That is closer to machine learning. But there is a catch: the employee’s judgment depends on the examples, the training, the feedback, and the clarity of the job. If the old examples were messy, inconsistent, or missing important cases, the employee may learn the wrong habits. AI has a similar issue. It can be useful, fast, and scalable, but it still needs clear tasks, good examples, feedback, and review. Concrete Example: Product Recommendations Suppose an online farm store wants to recommend products to customers. A traditional rule-based system might say: - If someone buys ground beef, recommend steaks. - If someone buys eggs, recommend chicken. - If someone buys pork chops, recommend bacon. That can work, but it is limited. A machine learning system could look at many past orders and learn patterns like: - Customers who buy breakfast sausage often buy eggs. - Customers who buy bulk freezer boxes often care about delivery timing. - Customers who buy lamb may also buy certain cuts of beef. - Customers who order every month behave differently from first-time customers. The model does not need to “understand” farming the way a human does. It finds patterns in order history and uses those patterns to predict what another customer may want. The business value comes from a practical outcome: better recommendations, larger baskets, happier customers, or fewer missed opportunities. But the recommendation still needs evaluation. If the model recommends out-of-stock items, ignores customer preferences, or pushes expensive products too aggressively, it may hurt trust. That is why AI is not just about making predictions. It is about making predictions that are useful, tested, and appropriate for the situation. Free Hands-On Exercise You can do this without any paid tools. Open a blank note and make three columns: 1.
