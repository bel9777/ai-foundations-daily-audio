AI Foundations — Day 24: Sequence Models before Transformers

Day 24 — Sequence Models Before Transformers Objective: Learn how machines handle data that comes in order—like words in a sentence, notes in a melody, or events in a log—using sequence models that came before transformers. Prerequisites: You should be comfortable with basic neural networks, layers and activations, backpropagation, training deep networks, and embeddings from Days 19–23. Why sequences are different Most machine learning examples are treated like one fixed-size input and one output. A sequence is different: it is data where order matters. If you change the order, you change the meaning. The sentence dog bites man is not the same as man bites dog . Sequence models are built to process one item at a time while keeping track of what came before. Before transformers, the main tools were recurrent neural networks , or RNNs , and their improved versions such as LSTMs and GRUs . 1) Recurrent neural networks: memory with a loop An RNN is a neural network that processes a sequence step by step. At each step, it takes two things: the current input item a hidden state , which is a compact summary of what the network has seen so far The hidden state is updated at every step and passed forward. Think of it as a notebook page you keep rewriting while reading a story. The page is never the whole story, but it carries the most useful notes from earlier words. This gives RNNs a simple kind of memory. But it is limited memory: important details from far back in the sequence can get forgotten. 2) Why plain RNNs struggle: vanishing and exploding gradients When training an RNN, the error signal has to travel backward through many time steps. That process is called backpropagation through time , meaning backpropagation applied across the steps of a sequence. Two common problems appear: Vanishing gradients : the training signal becomes tiny, so early steps learn very slowly or not at all. Exploding gradients : the training signal becomes too large and unstable. Here is a useful review from Day 21: backpropagation is the method for assigning credit for error. In sequence models, that credit assignment becomes harder because the network must figure out which earlier words or events mattered for a later prediction. And from Day 22: training reliably often requires careful gradient handling. In sequence models, techniques like gradient clipping—reducing gradients when they become too large—help keep training stable. 3) LSTMs and GRUs: better memory control To reduce the forgetting problem, researchers designed gated RNNs. A gate is a learned switch that decides how much information to keep, forget, or write. The two main gated variants are: LSTM , short for Long Short-Term Memory GRU , short for Gated Recurrent Unit Both use gates to manage memory more carefully than a plain RNN. In plain language: a gate can preserve important information longer a gate can drop irrelevant information a gate can help the network decide when to update its memory Analogy: Imagine reading a long recipe while cooking. A plain RNN is like trying to remember everything in your head. An LSTM is like having three sticky notes: one for ingredients to keep, one for steps to forget, and one for the next action to write down. 4) Sequence-to-one and sequence-to-sequence tasks Sequence models are used in different input/output shapes: Sequence-to-one : many inputs, one output. Example: predict whether a customer review is positive or negative. Sequence-to-sequence : many inputs, many outputs. Example: translate one sentence into another language. One-to-sequence : one input, many outputs. Example: generate a caption from an image, or produce a short melody from a starting note. A time step is one position in the sequence. At each time step, the model reads one item and updates its hidden state. In language, the items are often word embeddings, connecting today’s lesson to Day 23: instead of using raw words, we use vectors where similar meanings are close together in geometry. Real-world example: predicting the next word in a typed message Suppose your phone suggests the next word as you type: “I’m going to the...” A sequence model reads the words one by one, builds a hidden state, and estimates likely next words such as store , office , or park . It uses the order of the words because order changes meaning. A model that ignored order would miss that going to the matters more than just the bag of words. This is also where embeddings help. If the model has learned that store and market are similar in meaning, it can generalize better when it sees a new phrase. Why these models mattered before transformers Before transformers became dominant, RNNs, LSTMs, and GRUs were the standard way to model order. They were important because they gave neural networks a way to process variable-length sequences without forcing everything into one fixed-size input. Their main limitation is that they process items one at a time, which makes long-range dependencies harder and training slower than newer methods. But they are still valuable for understanding how sequence modeling evolved and for seeing why attention later became such an important idea. Free hands-on exercise Try this with only a notebook or text editor: Write five short sentences with the same words in different orders, such as cats chase mice , mice chase cats , and variations with other words. For each sentence, ask yourself: what changes when the word order changes? Now imagine reading each sentence one word at a time. After each word, write a one-line summary of what a hidden state might remember. Finally, choose one task: next-word prediction, sentiment detection, or sentence translation. Write one paragraph explaining why a sequence model helps. If you want an extra challenge, compare a plain RNN and an LSTM in your own words: what problem is the gate trying to solve?
