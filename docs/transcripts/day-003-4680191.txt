AI Foundations — Day 3: The Basic Machine Learning Workflow

Why This Matters

Most AI projects fail before the model matters, because the team never agrees on the job. Today gives you a map for turning a vague hope into a workable learning project. Whether you are sorting messages, planning inventory, or predicting demand, the same sequence helps: define the task, gather examples, train, check the result, and keep watching it. Here is the useful question: if a model made one decision for you tomorrow, what exact decision would it make? A clear answer turns a tempting technology idea into a practical starting point.

The Mental Model

Think of machine learning as training a new employee with a clear role and a feedback loop. You would not say, be smarter. You would name the task, show examples, explain what counts as a good result, and review early work. A model needs the same structure. First, define the task. It should say what goes in and what useful answer comes out. Predict whether an email needs a reply today is a task. Improve the business is not. Next, collect examples. In supervised learning, each example has inputs and a known answer, called a label. The model studies many of these pairs. Then comes preparation. Remove mistakes, duplicates, and information that would not truly be available when the model makes a future decision. Training is the practice stage. The model makes a guess, compares it with the known answer, receives a measure of error, and adjusts. That cycle repeats. Evaluation is different. It asks whether the model works on examples it did not practice on. A high score on familiar examples can mean memorization, not useful learning. If evaluation is weak, improve the data, the inputs, the model, or even the task itself. If it is good enough for the risk involved, deploy it in a real workflow. Finally, monitor it. Conditions change, and yesterday’s useful pattern can become tomorrow’s bad advice. The durable model is not a magic black box. It is a loop: define, collect, prepare, train, evaluate, improve, deploy, and monitor. Each step creates a question you can inspect. What are we deciding? Which facts are available? What result would be useful? How will we notice when the pattern stops helping? That discipline is what keeps a project connected to a real need.

A Real-World Example

Imagine the farm stand wants help deciding which items may sell out by closing time. Start with a precise task: for each item at opening, predict sold out by close, yes or no. Possible inputs include the item, price, day of week, weather, starting inventory, and recent sales. The label is the historical answer: did it sell out? Before training, check the records. Were prices written consistently? Did a holiday distort a typical Saturday? Did anyone enter the final inventory after the store closed, creating information that would not be known at opening? Then train a simple model on past days. Do not judge it on those same days alone. Hold out other days and ask how often it predicts the outcome correctly, and which mistakes hurt. Maybe missing a likely sellout is worse than carrying a little extra stock. If the results are good enough, use the model as a suggestion, not an automatic order. Each week, compare its suggestions with what really happened. A change in product mix, weather, or customer habits can weaken a once-helpful pattern. That is monitoring in plain language: keep checking the new employee after the job changes. Start with a simple baseline too. Compare the model with a plain rule, such as assume the usual Saturday items will sell out. If the model cannot beat that simple rule, it has not yet earned a place in the workflow.

Pause and Practice

Pause the episode and choose one decision from your work or daily life that could become a machine learning task. Say the task in one sentence. What information would the model see before deciding? What historical answer would tell you whether it was right? How would you test it on new examples? And what would a costly mistake look like? Take a moment before continuing. Now check your thinking. A useful task has a specific output, such as predict, classify, or estimate. Inputs are the facts available at decision time. The label is the past answer you want to learn from. Evaluation uses examples that were not part of training. And risk tells you how cautious the system should be. If your task still sounds broad, narrow it. Replace use AI for inventory with predict whether a specific item will sell out today. Clear tasks make every later choice easier. If you cannot name a reliable past answer, that is useful information too. It may mean the task needs a different measurement before a model can help.

Recap and One Action

Three takeaways. First, begin with a clear task, not a vague ambition. Second, training learns from examples, while evaluation checks whether learning transfers to new ones. Third, deployment is not the finish line; monitoring keeps the system honest as conditions change. One action for today: write down one decision you make repeatedly and complete four labels beside it: task, inputs, label, and risk. You do not need software yet. That small design exercise is the first real step in a useful machine learning project. Keep it concrete enough that another person could understand the decision without guessing.
