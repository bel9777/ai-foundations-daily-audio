AI Foundations — Day 32: Generative AI across Text, Images, and Audio

Day 32: Generative AI Across Text, Images, and Audio Objective: By the end of today’s lesson, you should understand how generative AI can create text, images, and audio, what these systems have in common, how they differ, and how to use them thoughtfully. Prerequisites: You should already be comfortable with the basic idea of transformers , tokens , context windows , pretraining , next-token prediction , fine-tuning , and human feedback from the last several lessons. 1) One idea, many media Generative AI is AI that produces new content instead of only classifying or detecting things. The same overall idea can be used for different modalities , which means types of data such as text, images, and audio. Although the outputs look different, the goal is similar in each case: learn patterns from examples and then generate something new that fits those patterns. Text generation produces words, sentences, or code. Image generation produces pictures. Audio generation produces sound, including speech, music, or effects. A useful way to think about this is that each model learns a kind of “compression” of patterns in its training data. Then, when given a prompt or starting point, it expands those patterns into a new output. 2) The common thread: predicting what comes next In the recent lessons, we studied next-token prediction , which is the training task where a model learns to guess the next token, or small piece of text, from the previous tokens. That lesson mainly focused on language models, but the same broad principle appears in other media too. For text, the next item is a token. For images, the model may learn to predict missing parts of an image or gradually build an image from noise. For audio, the model may predict the next sound segment or generate wave patterns that become speech or music. Important review: the model does not “understand” in the human sense. It learns statistical patterns from data. That is why the quality of the training data and the structure of the task matter so much. 3) How text, images, and audio are represented To generate anything, the model must first represent it in a form the computer can process. Text tokens are small pieces of text, such as words or word parts. Image patches are small regions of an image treated like units for processing. Audio samples are tiny measurements of sound over time. These are different kinds of building blocks, but they serve a similar purpose: they break a complex thing into manageable units. Analogy: Think of generative AI like a very versatile chef. For text, the chef cooks with words as ingredients. For images, the ingredients are visual pieces. For audio, the ingredients are sound fragments. The recipe changes, but the basic skill is the same: combine learned patterns into something coherent. 4) Three common generation approaches There are several ways generative models can create outputs. Here are three important ones in simple terms: Autoregressive generation : the model creates output step by step, each step depending on previous steps. This is common in text generation and also used in some image and audio systems. Diffusion : the model starts with noise, which means random static-like data, and gradually turns it into a clear image or sound by removing noise in many small steps. Seq2seq or sequence-to-sequence : the model converts one sequence into another, such as speech-to-text or text-to-speech, where input and output are both ordered data. These methods differ in mechanics, but all aim to generate outputs that match the prompt and the learned patterns. Review from Day 26: transformers are still central here because they are excellent at modeling relationships across a sequence. In many modern generative systems, the transformer is the engine that helps the model decide what comes next. 5) A concrete real-world example Imagine a small business owner who needs a simple marketing package: They ask a text model to draft a product description. They ask an image model to create a social media graphic for the product. They ask an audio model to generate a short spoken version of the ad for a podcast-style clip. This is a practical example of generative AI across modalities. One campaign can use three different media types, each generated by a specialized model. But the owner still needs to check the results. The text might sound persuasive but inaccurate, the image might contain odd details, and the audio might mispronounce a brand name. Generative AI can help with draft creation, but it does not replace review. 6) Why fine-tuning and human feedback still matter Fine-tuning , from Day 30, means training a model further on a smaller, targeted dataset so it performs better on a specific task. This matters across all modalities. A general model may be impressive, but a fine-tuned model can be better at a particular style, domain, or workflow. Human feedback , from Day 31, means people evaluate outputs and help shape model behavior toward more useful results. That is especially important when outputs must be safe, accurate, or on-brand. Human feedback can help a model produce better text, more usable images, or more natural audio. Important review: better prompts and better data can help, but they do not remove the need for judgment. Human review is still a key part of using generative AI responsibly. 7) Strengths and limits Generative AI is useful because it can speed up brainstorming, drafting, prototyping, and content variation. It can also help people who need a starting point rather than a finished product. Its limits are just as important: It can generate convincing but incorrect content. It can reflect bias in its training data. It may struggle with exact control, especially in images and audio. It can produce outputs that look polished but need fact-checking. The main skill for a beginner is not just using the tools, but checking the results carefully and knowing when human expertise is required. Free hands-on exercise Try this no-paid-tools exercise using only a notebook or a plain text editor: Pick one simple idea, such as “a bicycle repair shop opening a new location.” Write three prompts for three media types: Text prompt: Ask for a 3-sentence announcement. Image prompt: Describe a poster that would match the announcement. Audio prompt: Write a 15-second spoken script for a local ad. For each prompt, write down what you would want the output to include and what errors you would watch for. Then answer this question: which medium is easiest to control precisely, and why?
