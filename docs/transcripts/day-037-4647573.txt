AI Foundations — Day 37: Examples, Decomposition, and Structured Outputs

Why This Matters

An AI answer can be fluent and still leave you with more work. Maybe the tone is wrong. Maybe it skips a key detail. Maybe it gives you a wall of text when you needed a simple checklist. Today is about a practical way to reduce that friction. You will learn to show an example, split a larger request into smaller decisions, and ask for an output with a clear shape. Think about the last AI response you had to rewrite. Was the problem really the model, or did it have to guess what a useful answer looked like?

The Mental Model

The first idea is examples. An example is a sample input, output, or pattern that shows the model what good looks like. A request such as summarize this professionally leaves many choices open. How formal should it be? How long? What should it emphasize? One short example can demonstrate the tone and level of detail you want. It is like training wheels. Training wheels do not ride the bicycle for you, but they help you stay upright while you learn the pattern. The second idea is decomposition. That simply means taking one complicated task and turning it into smaller tasks. A large request often hides several decisions inside it. If you ask for a good email, the model must infer the issue, choose the important facts, decide on a tone, and pick an order. Instead, you can ask it to identify the issue, list the facts, draft the message, and then check whether the tone is firm and respectful. Each step is easier to inspect. The third idea is structured output. This means asking for a fixed, predictable format: a short list, a table, numbered steps, or labeled fields. If you request Issue, Cause, and Next Step, you make omissions easy to see. Structure is not decoration. It makes an answer easier to compare, reuse, and pass into another workflow. It also makes a useful handoff. A teammate can scan the same fields. A later tool can receive the same predictable pieces. You can compare two possible answers without first untangling their prose. The format should serve the decision you need to make. For a plan, choose actions, owners, and timing. For a risk review, choose issue, evidence, and next step. Together, the three ideas work like furniture assembly. The example is the finished chair beside you. Decomposition is the booklet broken into steps. Structure is the labeled parts list. A vague request to build it well gives no one much help.

A Real-World Example

Imagine you are preparing study notes for a history quiz. A vague prompt says, make me study notes on the French Revolution. That may work, but it leaves the model to choose what counts as important and how to organize it. A stronger request uses all three tools. First, give a tiny example of the preferred style, such as Cause — economic hardship made people angry. Then decompose the work: first list five causes, then five major events, then three outcomes. Finally, require a structure: use three headings with bullet points underneath. Now the answer has a visible standard. You can check whether each part is present. You can see whether a fact belongs in causes, events, or outcomes. And you can revise one part without starting over. The same approach fits ordinary work. If you need a customer update for the farm, first have the AI identify the delivery change, then write the message, then return a subject line and a two-sentence body. If you need a meeting brief, ask for decisions, risks, and next steps. A shape that matches the real use makes the result more useful.

Pause and Practice

Try this with one task you already have. It could be planning groceries, organizing a weekend project, drafting an apology text, or preparing interview questions. First, write the vague version you would normally type. Second, add one small example of the tone or format you want. Third, break the task into three smaller steps and ask for a predictable output. Pause the episode now and draft those three versions. When you return, ask yourself three questions. What is the main purpose of an example? It shows the model what good looks like, reducing guesswork. What does decomposition mean? It means breaking one larger job into smaller, easier jobs. Why use structure? It makes the response easier to read, inspect, compare, and reuse. Remember that examples and structure do not replace clear instructions, context, and constraints from yesterday. They strengthen them. And because a polished answer can still contain mistakes, smaller, visible pieces also make checking easier.

Recap and One Action

Three takeaways. First, examples demonstrate the shape, tone, or detail you want, so the model has less to guess. Second, decomposition turns a complicated request into smaller decisions you can verify. Third, structured output makes missing pieces and weak reasoning easier to spot. Your action for today is simple: choose one real prompt you expect to use this week. Add one example, split it into three steps, and specify the response format. Then compare it with the vague version. Notice which addition made the greatest difference, and keep that prompt as a reusable starting point. Better AI output often starts with a better request: show the pattern, split the task, and ask for a form you can actually use.
