AI Foundations — Day 5: Data Is the Model's Experience

Why This Matters

A model does not learn from the whole world. It learns from the examples you choose, measure, and label. That means data is not a background detail; it is the model’s experience. Today you will learn how to inspect that experience before trusting a prediction. Ask one practical question: at the moment a real decision is made, does this dataset contain the right information, from the right situations, without secretly revealing the answer?

The Mental Model

Picture teaching someone about farm operations with a photo album. Their understanding depends on which photos are included, what each photo shows, and which captions explain the outcome. If the album contains only sunny days, they may learn the wrong lesson about difficult weather. If every successful field happens to show a red tractor, they might mistake a coincidence for a cause. A dataset works the same way. Each example is one case, such as one completed delivery. Features are the inputs the model can inspect, including planned distance, departure time, forecast weather, or number of stops. The target, also called a label, is the outcome to predict, such as late or on time. Representation is the form those real-world details take inside the data. A model does not experience a rainy morning; it receives values such as rainfall, temperature, hour, and road condition. Data quality asks whether those records are accurate, complete, consistent, and correctly labeled. Representativeness asks a different question: do the records cover the conditions where the model will be used? A perfect collection of summer deliveries can still be a poor basis for year-round decisions. Bias is a systematic distortion in what gets collected, measured, labeled, or left out. Leakage is even more dangerous: it occurs when training or testing includes information that would not exist at the real prediction moment. In short, recreate the information boundary of the real decision. The model can only learn responsibly from evidence it would actually have. A useful data review therefore happens before model training, not after a surprising result. It asks who and what appears in the records, which decisions created those records, and which ordinary cases were never recorded at all. It also checks the timing of every feature. The key discipline is not collecting every imaginable column. It is selecting evidence that is available, meaningful, and honest for the decision the system will face.

A Real-World Example

Suppose you want to predict, at departure time, whether a delivery will arrive more than twenty minutes late. One example is one historical delivery. Reasonable features include planned distance, scheduled departure, actual departure delay, forecast weather, number of stops, vehicle category, and recent route duration. The target is whether the promised arrival time was missed by more than twenty minutes. Now audit the dataset. Are arrival and departure times accurate and in consistent time zones? Does it include winter, holidays, new routes, and unusually large orders? Are canceled deliveries missing, making the historical record look easier than the real work? Most important, does any field reveal the outcome after the fact? Actual arrival time clearly cannot be an input at departure. Notes written after the delivery can also leak the answer. Even a clean split can mislead if repeated records from the same customer appear in training and testing. Grouping related cases can make the evaluation more honest. More records do not fix a misleading boundary. A large dataset can still teach a model the wrong shortcut. The responsible test matches the decision moment, then checks performance across meaningful slices instead of trusting only an overall average.

Pause and Practice

Pause now and choose a prediction that could help with a real decision: a late delivery, equipment failure, customer reorder, or household energy use. Write six lines. First, name the decision. Second, say what one example represents. Third, define the target and when it must be predicted. Fourth, list three features genuinely available then. Fifth, name one coverage risk, such as a season, location, or rare event missing from the data. Sixth, name one tempting field that might leak the answer. Pause the episode and do it. Here are the retrieval answers. A feature is an input available to the model; a target is the outcome it must predict. High-quality data can still be unrepresentative if accurate records omit important operating conditions. Leakage makes a weak model look strong by giving it answer-revealing information it will not have in real use. Your final line should be: I would not trust this model until I tested this risk.

Recap and One Action

Three takeaways. First, a dataset is the selected slice of reality a model experiences, so features, targets, and representation shape what it can learn. Second, quality and representativeness are different: accurate records may still miss critical conditions. Third, leakage creates false confidence by letting the model see information too late for the real decision. One action for today: make a mini data card for one prediction you care about. State the decision, example, target, available features, coverage risk, and leakage risk. That small audit is often more valuable than immediately training a model. Review it before you collect data. Use it before training a model.
