AI Foundations — Day 8: Learning without Labels

Day 8: Learning without Labels Objective: Understand how machines can learn useful structure from data when we do not tell them the correct answer for each example. Prerequisites: You should already be comfortable with basic machine learning ideas from Days 2–7: patterns and predictions, the general workflow, generalization, labeled examples, and classification versus regression. Why this matters So far, much of our focus has been on supervised learning , which means learning from labeled data —examples paired with correct answers. Today we look at a different situation: the data has no labels. The machine must discover structure on its own. This is called unsupervised learning . Unsupervised learning means finding patterns, groups, or relationships in data without being told the target answer in advance. That may sound harder, but it is also closer to how we often meet the world in real life. Before someone tells you what counts as “normal” in a dataset, you can still notice clusters, similarities, and unusual cases. 1) What “learning without labels” means In supervised learning, the model sees inputs and correct outputs. In unsupervised learning, the model sees only the inputs. It tries to answer questions like: Which examples are similar? Are there natural groups in the data? What stands out as unusual? Can we summarize a lot of information in a simpler way? Notice how this connects to patterns from Day 2. The model is still looking for regularities—it just does not have labels telling it what to predict. 2) Clustering: grouping similar things The most common unsupervised idea is clustering . Clustering means grouping items so that items in the same group are more similar to each other than to items in other groups. Think of clustering as sorting a messy pile of socks into piles by color, size, or texture—without being told the “correct” piles ahead of time. The socks themselves suggest the groups. A model does something similar with data. For example, a shopping company might group customers by buying behavior. One cluster might contain people who buy baby products often; another might include people who buy sports equipment; another might include people who shop mostly during sales. Memorable analogy: Clustering is like entering a room full of mixed puzzle pieces and asking, “Which pieces seem to belong together?” You are not solving the full puzzle yet. You are first organizing the pieces by shape, color, and edge style so the bigger task becomes easier. 3) Similarity: how the model decides what belongs together To cluster data, the model needs a way to measure how close or alike two items are. That is called similarity . Similarity means a numerical way to express how much two things resemble each other. If two customers spend money in very similar ways, their data points may be considered close. If two handwritten digits look alike, they may also be considered similar. The exact definition of similarity depends on the data and the goal. This is where Day 7 comes back into the picture. A decision boundary is a line or surface used in classification to separate labeled classes. In unsupervised learning, there usually is no labeled boundary at the start. Instead, the model is trying to discover where natural groupings might be. Later, those discovered groups can sometimes be used to build a decision boundary for a supervised task. 4) Dimensionality reduction: simplifying data without losing the main idea Another unsupervised idea is dimensionality reduction . Dimensionality reduction means compressing data into fewer measurements while keeping the most important structure. Here, dimension means one measurable aspect of data. For example, a house might have dimensions like size, number of bedrooms, age, and distance to work. More dimensions can mean more detail, but also more complexity. Why reduce dimensions? Because real data can be too large to inspect directly. Dimensionality reduction helps reveal the main shape of the data, remove noise, and make patterns easier to see. It is a bit like taking a long paragraph and writing a short summary that keeps the central meaning. This also connects to Day 5: data is the model’s experience . If the experience is messy and high-dimensional, unsupervised methods can help reveal its structure before we build a more specific model. 5) Anomaly detection: finding what does not fit A third unsupervised idea is anomaly detection . Anomaly detection means identifying data points that look unusual compared with the rest of the data. If most credit card purchases are small and local, but one transaction is very large and far away, that transaction may be an anomaly. An anomaly is not always bad or wrong, but it deserves attention. This matters in real systems because unusual patterns can mean fraud, equipment failure, data entry errors, or simply rare but important events. Unsupervised learning is useful here because we often do not have enough labeled examples of the unusual cases. Real-world example: organizing news articles Imagine a news website with thousands of unlabeled articles. Nobody has tagged each article by topic. An unsupervised system could cluster articles based on word patterns. One cluster might contain sports stories, another politics, another technology. The system is not “understanding” the articles like a person does, but it can still discover that some articles belong together. That discovered structure can help people browse content, recommend related articles, or prepare labeled data for a later supervised model. How this fits the bigger machine learning workflow Let’s connect today’s lesson to earlier days: From Day 3, remember the workflow: define the task, gather data, train, evaluate, and improve. In unsupervised learning, the “task” may be discovery rather than prediction. From Day 4, remember generalization. Even without labels, we still want patterns that hold beyond one dataset. A cluster that only exists because of a random glitch is not useful. From Day 6, remember that labels tell the model what outcome to learn. Today we saw what happens when labels are missing: the model can still search for structure, but the goal is different. Free hands-on exercise: cluster your own data You do not need paid tools for this. Use paper, a notes app, or a spreadsheet. Step 1: Make a list of 15 everyday items from your life. Examples: books you own, foods you eat often, apps you use, or tasks you do in a week. Step 2: Choose two or three simple properties for each item. For books, properties might be fiction/nonfiction , short/long , and paperback/hardcover . For foods, properties might be sweet/savory , hot/cold , and snack/meal . Step 3: Group the items into clusters by similarity. Do this by hand. Write down why each item belongs in a group. Step 4: Look for anything that does not fit well. Those are your possible anomalies. Step 5: Reflect: Did you have to invent the grouping rule yourself? That is the key idea. Without labels, you had to decide what “similar” means. Knowledge check 1. What is unsupervised learning? 2.
