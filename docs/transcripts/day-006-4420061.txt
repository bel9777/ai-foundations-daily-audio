AI Foundations — Day 6: Learning from Labeled Examples

Why This Matters

Many important decisions are learned from examples that already have answers: which emails are spam, which plants look diseased, or which deliveries are likely to be late. Today you will learn how supervised learning turns those solved examples into feedback for a model. Keep this question in mind: if you wanted a system to make a useful prediction tomorrow, what answer would you need to attach to its examples today?

The Mental Model

Supervised learning is like studying with flash cards that have an answer key. Each training example contains an input and a target. The input is the evidence the model sees. The target is the answer it is supposed to learn to predict. A leaf photo might map to healthy or diseased. House details might map to a sale price. Email text might map to spam or not spam. The known answer is often called a label, especially when it is a category. These labels turn examples into feedback. The model makes a prediction, compares it with the target, and changes its adjustable settings so future predictions can be better. Those adjustable settings are parameters. Picture a machine with many knobs. During training, an optimizer adjusts the knobs in a direction expected to reduce error. The error is measured by a loss function. Loss is a training signal: it converts the difference between a prediction and the target into a number. Lower loss means the model fits those examples better according to that measurement. Classification and regression are the two common forms. Classification predicts a category, such as sellable or damaged. Regression predicts a number, such as a delivery time or price. The output and the measurement of error differ, but the learning loop is similar: predict, compare, adjust, repeat. One pass through the dataset is an epoch. Over many epochs, training performance can improve. But success on seen examples is not the finish line. Validation asks whether the model learned a pattern that works on new examples. A model can memorize accidental clues. If every diseased leaf photo has a blue tray in the background, it may learn blue tray rather than disease. That can look successful during training and fail later. Labels matter just as much as the model. They can be wrong, inconsistent, subjective, or delayed. That is label noise. A large dataset with unreliable labels can teach the wrong lesson very efficiently. The model learns from the experience it receives, not from the truth you hoped it would discover.

A Real-World Example

Imagine a farm sorting apples from photos into sellable or damaged. Trained staff label examples, so each record is an apple image paired with the desired category. The model predicts a probability of damage. When it confidently disagrees with the staff label, the loss is high, and training adjusts its parameters. Before using the system, the team tests it on apples from different harvest days, cameras, lighting, varieties, and workers. That matters because near-duplicate photos could make a random validation split look excellent while hiding failure in a new season. The operation must also choose a threshold. Rejecting an apple above a seventy percent damage prediction is not a fact learned by the model; it is a decision about tradeoffs. A lower threshold catches more damaged apples but rejects more good ones. This separates three jobs: define the examples and labels, train the model to reduce prediction error, and decide how to use its predictions in the real operation. The business metric may care about waste, missed damage, or time saved, while the training loss is simply the signal used to adjust the model.

Pause and Practice

Pause the episode and become the training algorithm for a moment. Imagine six examples for a fictional plant: soil feels dry, leaves droop, hot day, and needs water. Start with a rule that predicts yes only when leaves droop. Compare it with the labels and count mistakes. Then change the rule to predict yes when soil feels dry, and count again. Pause now and try it on paper. Here are the key answers. In the lesson’s examples, the drooping rule makes two mistakes, while the dry-soil rule fits all six training examples. But do not trust that result completely. An unseen plant could have wet soil and drooping leaves because it was recently transplanted. Low training error shows fit to the examples. It does not prove generalization. Also answer these retrieval questions: supervised means the examples include known targets; a loss function trains the model, while a business metric judges usefulness; falling training loss with worsening validation is the usual sign of overfitting.

Recap and One Action

Three takeaways. First, supervised learning uses input-target pairs, and labels provide the feedback. Second, models learn through a repeated predict, compare, adjust loop guided by loss. Third, validation and label quality protect you from mistaking memorization for useful learning. One action for today: choose one prediction you care about and write one possible input, one target, and one real-world metric. Then ask what could make the labels wrong or unrepresentative. That simple check connects the model’s training signal to the decision you actually need to make. Do it before trusting a polished result.
