AI Foundations — Day 29: Next-Token Prediction and Emergent Capabilities

Day 29: Next-Token Prediction and Emergent Capabilities Objective: Understand what a language model is actually trained to do, why predicting the next token can produce surprisingly broad abilities, and where those abilities come from. Prerequisites: You should already be comfortable with tokens, context windows, transformers, and the basic idea of pretraining from large text corpora. Yesterday we saw that pretraining teaches a model from massive amounts of text. Today we answer the key question: what is the learning task? For most large language models, the task is simple to state: given the previous tokens, predict the next token. 1) Next-token prediction: the core training task Next-token prediction means the model reads a sequence of tokens and tries to guess the most likely next one. A token is a chunk of text, such as a word, part of a word, punctuation mark, or symbol. Example: if the text is The cat sat on the , the model might assign high probability to mat , floor , or couch . During training, the model compares its guesses to the actual next token in the text and adjusts its internal settings to do better next time. This is a form of supervised learning , meaning the model learns from examples with correct answers already present in the data. Here, the “answer” is simply the next token that really appeared in the text. 2) Why such a simple task teaches so much At first, next-token prediction sounds narrow. But to predict well, the model must pick up many patterns at once: Grammar : which word forms fit together. Facts : what text tends to say about the world. Style : whether the passage sounds formal, casual, technical, or poetic. Reasoning patterns : how explanations, comparisons, lists, and arguments are usually written. Why? Because language is not random. Text written by people contains structure. To guess the next token, the model must learn the structure that makes the text plausible. Analogy: Imagine reading the next line of a long detective novel with the page covered except for the last few sentences. To guess what comes next, you must track characters, motives, setting, and writing style. The model is doing something similar across enormous amounts of text. 3) Emergent capabilities: skills that appear later than expected Emergent capabilities are abilities that are weak or absent in smaller models but become noticeably better in larger models, sometimes quite suddenly. The word emergent means the capability seems to appear from the interaction of many parts, rather than being explicitly programmed in one place. Examples people often point to include: Following multi-step instructions more reliably. Solving harder word problems. Translating between languages better. Keeping track of longer patterns in text. Important caution: “emergent” does not mean magical. It usually means that as models get larger and training improves, their performance crosses a threshold where a skill becomes useful enough to notice. The underlying behavior is still the result of next-token prediction. 4) Why these capabilities can seem surprising A model is not told, “Learn algebra,” or “Learn how to summarize.” Instead, it sees huge amounts of text where people solve problems, explain ideas, and answer questions. By trying to predict the next token in those examples, the model learns patterns that support those tasks. For instance, if the text often contains steps like “first,” “therefore,” and “because,” the model learns the shapes of explanations. If it sees many worked examples, it can imitate the structure of those examples when prompted with a similar problem. This is one reason the same base model can appear to do many different things. The abilities are not separate tools inside the model; they are skills that arise from learning general text patterns. 5) Real-world example: customer support drafts Suppose a company trains or uses a pretrained language model to help draft customer support replies. A customer writes, “I was charged twice for my order.” The model can generate a helpful response because it has learned common response patterns from text: apologize, acknowledge the issue, ask for details, and suggest next steps. The model is not “understanding” the situation like a human agent would, but it has learned a statistically strong pattern for what a good reply looks like in similar contexts. That is next-token prediction in action. Review from earlier lessons Let’s connect this to two earlier ideas: Embeddings from Day 23: tokens are represented as vectors, which means the model can compare and combine meanings in a numeric space. Attention from Day 25: the model can focus on the most relevant earlier tokens in the context window when predicting the next one. Those pieces help the transformer turn a sequence of tokens into a strong next-token guess. The architecture from Day 26 provides the machinery; the context window from Day 27 sets how much prior text can be considered; and pretraining from Day 28 provides the large-scale learning experience. Free hands-on exercise Do this with a notebook and any text you already have access to, such as a news article, a recipe, or a short story. Copy one paragraph of text. Cover the last word of each sentence and try to predict it yourself before uncovering it. Now cover a few more words and predict the next token or word using context only. Ask: what clues helped most—grammar, topic, style, or logic? Then repeat with a very different kind of text, such as a recipe or a legal notice. Notice how your predictions change when the style changes. This is a simple way to feel how next-token prediction depends on context, not just isolated words. Knowledge check 1) What is the basic training objective of a typical language model? 2) Why can next-token prediction lead to abilities like writing and problem solving? 3) What does “emergent capability” mean in this course? ------------------------------ Answers 1) Predict the next token given the previous tokens. 2) Because to predict text well, the model must learn grammar, facts, style, and common reasoning patterns that appear in the training text. 3) A skill that becomes noticeably better, or appears to “show up,” as the model gets larger or better trained, even though it is still the result of next-token prediction.
