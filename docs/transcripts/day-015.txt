AI Foundations — Day 15: Baselines: The Simplest Useful Benchmark

Day 15: Baselines — The Simplest Useful Benchmark Objective: Learn how to build and use a baseline, compare your model against it, and avoid being fooled by numbers that look good but mean little. Prerequisites: You should be comfortable with features, loss functions, optimization, evaluation metrics, confusion matrices, and cross-validation from Days 9–14. Before we talk about clever models, we need something simpler: a baseline . A baseline is the simplest reasonable method you can use to solve a task. It gives you a starting point for comparison. If your model cannot beat a baseline, it may be adding complexity without adding value. 1) Why baselines matter Suppose a model predicts whether an email is spam. If it scores 92% accuracy, that sounds strong. But what if 91% of emails are not spam? Then a dumb rule that says “always predict not spam” already gets 91% accuracy. In that case, the model is only barely better than a trivial guess. The baseline tells you whether the improvement is real and useful. This is the first idea: good evaluation is relative . A metric by itself can mislead you. You need a comparison point. 2) Common kinds of baselines A trivial baseline is the simplest possible rule. Examples: Majority-class baseline : always predict the most common class. A class is a category label, such as spam or not spam. Mean baseline : for a number prediction task, always predict the average value from the training data. Last-value baseline : for time-based data, predict that the next value will be the same as the most recent one. A reasonable baseline is still simple, but slightly smarter than the trivial one. For example, in a housing-price task, a baseline might predict price using only square footage, or use a small linear model with one or two obvious features. The point is not to be fancy. The point is to establish a useful floor. 3) Baselines and your evaluation metric Earlier, we talked about evaluation metrics —the numbers we use to judge performance. A baseline should be measured with the same metric as the model you want to test. If you care about precision or recall , compare those values too, not just accuracy. Remember the confusion matrix from Day 13? It counts true positives , false positives , true negatives , and false negatives . A baseline can reveal tradeoffs that accuracy hides. For example, the “always negative” baseline may have high accuracy in a rare-disease setting, but its recall for positive cases is zero. That means it misses every real case, which is usually unacceptable. This is the second review idea: the metric must match the goal . A baseline is only helpful if you compare it using the right measure. 4) Cross-validation makes the comparison trustworthy From Day 14, you learned cross-validation , which means evaluating on several different train/test splits and averaging the results. This matters for baselines too. A single lucky split can make a weak model look strong, or a strong model look weak. If you compute your baseline and your model on the same cross-validation splits, you get a more reliable comparison. That way, you are comparing them under the same conditions, not letting randomness decide the winner. This is the third review idea: reliable comparisons come from repeated evaluation , not from one impressive number. 5) A memorable analogy: the ruler before the sculpture Think of a baseline like a ruler before you start carving a sculpture. The ruler does not create art. It simply tells you how much material you have removed and whether your work is improving. Without the ruler, you might admire a shape that is actually no better than the original block. A baseline plays the same role in machine learning: it measures whether your model is truly shaping something useful. Concrete example: predicting house prices Imagine a beginner wants to predict home prices. A simple baseline could be: Mean baseline: predict the average sale price for every house. If the average price in the training data is $300,000, the baseline predicts $300,000 for every home. You then compute a loss such as mean absolute error , which is the average size of the prediction mistakes. If your fancy model only improves a little over the mean baseline, you know the extra complexity may not be worth it yet. Now suppose you add features like square footage, number of bedrooms, and neighborhood. If the model clearly beats the baseline across cross-validation splits, that is evidence the features are useful and the model is learning something real. When a baseline is good enough Sometimes the best model is not the most complex one. A baseline can be enough when: The task is simple. The data is noisy or limited. Interpretability matters more than raw performance. A more complex model does not beat the baseline by a meaningful amount. That last point is important: a tiny numerical improvement may not matter in practice. A baseline helps you ask, “Is this improvement worth the added cost, risk, or complexity?” Free hands-on exercise You can do this with paper, a spreadsheet, or a free note app—no paid tools needed. Choose a simple prediction task from your life or a public habit you can estimate, such as tomorrow’s temperature, whether you will exercise, or whether an email is spam. Write down 20 examples you can reasonably estimate or label. Create a baseline: For a category task, use the most common label. For a number task, use the average of the previous examples. Judge it with one metric that fits the task, such as accuracy, precision, recall, or average absolute error. If you can, compare it to a slightly smarter rule using one extra piece of information. Write one sentence answering: Did the smarter rule beat the baseline enough to matter? Three questions to check your understanding What is a baseline in machine learning? Why should a baseline be evaluated with the same metric you care about for the model? Why is cross-validation useful when comparing a model to a baseline? ------------------------------ Answers 1) A baseline is the simplest reasonable method for a task, used as a comparison point for more complex models. 2) Because the metric should reflect the real goal. If you care about recall or error size, accuracy alone may hide important failures. 3) Cross-validation reduces the chance that one lucky or unlucky data split gives a misleading comparison.
