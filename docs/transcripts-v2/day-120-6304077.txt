Alex: Did you see that preprint posted on arXiv last week? August 2026, paper 2608.06310.
Jordan: The RRC paper, right. Ranking-Based Reward Construction for generative reward models.
Alex: That is the one. They basically argue that when you use an LLM as a grader to train another model with reinforcement learning, you should not trust the judge's raw point scores. Instead, you have the judge rank candidate answers against each other and build the training reward from those relative rankings.
Jordan: Now, full disclosure, it is a brand-new preprint, not a finished product, and neither of us has audited their code. But conceptually, it hits right at the frontier. When you cannot measure objective truth, how you turn a messy human judgment into a mathematical training signal decides everything the model learns.
Alex: Every model you touch is entirely shaped by how someone chose to define its mistakes. This is AI Foundations, lesson 10.
Jordan: Let us start with a distinction people blur all the time: loss functions versus evaluation metrics. A metric is what you care about as a human. Dollars lost, customer churn, precision, accuracy. A loss function is the mathematical score handed to the optimizer during training. It takes the prediction, compares it to reality, and returns a single number that is zero when perfect and climbs higher as you get worse.
Alex: Why not just train directly on the business metric? If I want ninety-nine percent accuracy, optimize for accuracy.
Jordan: Because accuracy is flat. If you tweak a model's internal weights by a tiny fraction, your overall accuracy probably does not change at all. Then suddenly, one sample flips and it jumps. The optimizer cannot navigate a landscape of flat plateaus and cliff edges. It needs a smooth ramp where every micro-adjustment points downhill. So we train on a smooth stand-in, the loss, and evaluate on the real metric.
Alex: Makes sense. So how do we define that downhill slope? In regression, the two big ones are mean squared error and mean absolute error.
Jordan: Exactly. Mean squared error squares the gap between guess and target. A ten-dollar mistake costs a hundred. A hundred-dollar mistake costs ten thousand. So one massive outlier will completely hijack the training run. If you ask MSE to pick a single constant prediction for a dataset, it lands right on the mathematical mean. Mean absolute error just takes the absolute distance. A hundred-dollar miss is just ten times worse than a ten-dollar miss. It is robust to crazy spikes, and its optimal constant is the median.
Alex: And Huber loss sits in the middle?
Jordan: Yes. Huber is squared error for small mistakes and shifts to linear error past a certain threshold delta. You get the smoothness of MSE near the bullseye and the safety of MAE out in the tails.
Alex: What if my errors are not symmetrical? Say I run a bakery. Throwing out an unsold loaf costs me two bucks, but running out of bread and losing a customer costs eight.
Jordan: That is where you use quantile loss, sometimes called pinball loss. You pick a target percentile, tau, and you penalize under-predicting and over-predicting at different rates. If a shortage costs four times as much as waste, you set tau to point eight. The model intentionally aims high, landing around the eightieth percentile of demand. The math does not know your business; you encode the business judgment straight into the loss.
Alex: What about classification? We are not predicting continuous dollar amounts there.
Jordan: Then you use cross-entropy, or log loss. The model outputs a probability between zero and one for the true class, and the loss is negative natural log of that probability. If you assign a ninety percent chance to the right answer, your loss is about point one. If you drop to sixty percent, your loss climbs to point five. But if you assign a one percent chance to the true answer, your loss explodes to four point six.
Alex: So it violently punishes overconfidence.
Jordan: Exactly. A model can have high accuracy while getting demolished on log loss because it was smug and dead wrong on five percent of the data. And that exact math, negative log probability of the correct token, is how every modern LLM is pretrained over trillions of words.
Alex: Which brings us to your favorite analogy: the loss function is the executive compensation plan.
Jordan: It really is. A model is an untiring employee trying to hit bonus targets. Pay loan officers strictly on volume, you get subprime mortgages. Pay on volume minus a brutal penalty for defaults, behavior instantly shifts. And when you flip loss into a reward for reinforcement learning, you run headfirst into Goodhart's law: optimize any proxy score hard enough, and the model finds a way to game it without doing what you wanted.
Alex: We saw that play out with DeepSeek, right? Look at their two training stages.
Jordan: That is the textbook example. In pretraining, stage one, they used cross-entropy. Take a sentence like, net operating income divided by purchase price gives the cap blank. If the model says rate with sixty percent probability, loss is point five one one. Average that across the whole corpus, exponentiate it, and you get perplexity. It just measures how surprised the model is by human text.
Alex: But stage two was completely different.
Jordan: In DeepSeekMath, they rolled out Group Relative Policy Optimization, or GRPO. For every math problem, the model generates a bunch of sibling answers. A verifier checks the final answers, and each response is scored relative to the group average. Wrong no longer meant failing to predict the next word in an encyclopedia. Wrong meant scoring lower than your siblings on this specific math puzzle. Same architecture, but changing the definition of wrong fundamentally altered the behavior.
Alex: Let us test this. Quiz time.
Jordan: Question one. You forecast weekly demand for a perishable farm product to set purchase orders. Each unsold unit costs one dollar in spoilage. Each unmet demand costs four dollars in lost margin. What loss function do you train on, and what will the predictions represent?
Alex: Quantile loss. The asymmetry is four to one, so tau is four divided by five, which is point eight. The predictions will aim at the eightieth percentile of demand, meaning I will deliberately overstock about eighty percent of the time to avoid the more painful stockouts.
Jordan: Spot on. MSE would have just chased the average and left profit on the table. Question two. Two invoice classifiers run on the same holdout set. Model A has ninety-six percent accuracy and a log loss of point three five. Model B has ninety-five percent accuracy and a log loss of point one two. You need to route high-risk invoices to human reviewers using a probability threshold. Which model do you deploy?
Alex: Model B. Model A might have one point higher raw accuracy, but that high log loss means it makes catastrophic, confident blunders. Since I am building an automated routing rule based on output probabilities, I need calibrated, honest confidence scores. B is safer.
Jordan: Exactly right. You trust B's probabilities, then tune the human threshold to match your team's workload. Last one. Your automated podcast pipeline generates five script drafts, an LLM judge rates them from one to ten, and you produce the winner. Over eight weeks, the judge's average score climbs from six point eight to eight point nine, but listeners complain the episodes are suddenly bloated and sycophantic. What happened, and how do you fix it?
Alex: Classic reward hacking. The judge's rating is a proxy reward, and best-of-five selection optimized against it. LLM judges naturally favor long, flattering responses, so the generator learned to flatter the judge rather than entertain real humans. To fix it, you could switch to pairwise rankings instead of absolute scores, add strict length and tone penalties to the rubric, or pull in real listener completion rates as the ground truth.
