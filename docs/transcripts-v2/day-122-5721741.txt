Alex: Did you see the chaos around Terminal-Bench 4.0 last month?
Jordan: The agent benchmark release in September? Yeah. The team said they calibrated compute limits, patched broken tasks, and retired saturated ones. But nobody can even agree on what the benchmark actually is right now.
Alex: Right. Epoch AI's review says it dropped eight tasks and tweaked twenty, while other trackers describe it as a sixty-six task test. And the scores are completely wild. Artificial Analysis puts Kimi K3.1 Pro at over sixty-seven percent, Anthropic self-reports Claude Sonnet 5.5 at over seventy percent, and then Vals AI runs the same benchmark and has most models sitting below thirty.
Jordan: That is the whole problem in a nutshell. A benchmark score is not a universal constant. It is tied to the harness, the compute limits, the retry rules, and the exact task version. If any of those shift, the number changes completely.
Alex: Which means if you don't know how to define a metric for your own business problem, you are basically flying blind on someone else's broken instrument panel. This is AI Foundations, lesson 12.
Jordan: Today we are connecting the value of a business decision directly to the math you use to measure it.
Alex: Where do people usually get that wrong?
Jordan: They start with the model's loss, or they just grab accuracy off the shelf. But remember from lessons ten and eleven, loss is just a smooth, continuous curve that gradient descent uses to update weights. Your business does not operate on smooth cross-entropy. It operates on step functions. Did the invoice get approved? Did the agent crash the database?
Alex: So you train on loss, but you evaluate on business impact.
Jordan: Exactly. Start with the decision. Say your system flags suspicious transactions for review. You have to price the outcomes. What does a missed fraud cost in real dollars? That is your cost of a false negative. What does a false alarm cost in wasted staff time? That is your cost of a false positive. Your true metric is cost-weighted error: false negatives times their cost, plus false positives times their cost.
Alex: And you also have a real-world bottleneck. A team can only review so many flags a day.
Jordan: Right. That is where capacity-bound metrics come in. If your analyst can only inspect fifteen items a day, calculating F1 score across every possible threshold is completely irrelevant. You care about precision at fifteen, or recall at fifteen. Pick your top fifteen highest-confidence flags and measure how many were actual problems.
Alex: That makes total sense for one-off decisions. But what about agents that execute multi-step workflows over time?
Jordan: That brings us to reliability metrics. You will see two flavors in the literature: pass at k, and pass to the power of k. Chen and the Codex team introduced pass at k back in 2021. It asks: if the model takes k shots at a task, does at least one succeed? That makes sense when a human is pairing with Claude Code and just needs one working solution out of three suggestions.
Alex: But terrible if the script runs alone at midnight.
Jordan: Exactly. For unattended automation, you need pass to the power of k, which Sierra introduced in tau-bench. That measures the probability that the agent succeeds on all k runs. If an agent has a ninety percent success rate on a single run, its reliability across a five-day work week is zero point nine to the fifth power. That drops your success rate to about fifty-nine percent. If you run it unattended, you will be fixing broken runs almost every week.
Alex: It is like that finance analogy you mentioned earlier.
Jordan: The loan covenant, yeah. A bank wants to know if a borrower can pay them back, but they cannot see the future. So they require the borrower to maintain a specific debt service coverage ratio. That ratio is just a proxy. If the borrower cuts essential building maintenance to inflate net operating income, they hit the covenant target while destroying the asset. That is Goodhart's law. When you treat the proxy as the goal, the model will game it.
Alex: We saw that happen in real time with SWE-bench Verified earlier this year, right?
Jordan: That was the textbook case. OpenAI published a note explaining why they retired it. For a couple of years, passing unit tests on real GitHub issues was our best proxy for software engineering skill. But over time, the test set leaked into training data. Worse, when researchers audited the unsolved tasks, over sixty percent were broken. Dozens of tasks had unit tests so rigid that they rejected totally valid bug fixes. The benchmark was actively penalizing correct answers.
Alex: And at the high end, it saturated at ninety-four percent, where a two-point difference is just statistical noise anyway.
Jordan: Precisely. Let's see if you can put these principles into practice. Pop quiz time.

Jordan: Scenario one. Your invoice detector processes six hundred invoices a month. Roughly two percent are true exceptions. Your controller can only review twenty flagged invoices a month. Model A has ninety-eight point five percent accuracy. Model B has ninety-six percent. What information do you need to pick between them, and what metric do you report to the controller?
Alex: First, throw out raw accuracy. With only twelve true exceptions out of six hundred invoices, a dumb model that flags literally zero invoices gets ninety-eight percent accuracy. I need to know the dollar cost of a missed exception versus the reviewer's hourly cost, plus the ranked scores from each model. To the controller, I report recall at twenty—meaning how many of those twelve real exceptions made it into their top twenty queue—and precision at twenty, along with the expected monthly dollar loss. Model B could easily be the winner there.
Jordan: Spot on. Scenario two. A vendor tells you their model beats a rival ninety-one to eighty-nine on a two-hundred-task benchmark. Another leaderboard shows them reversed. Why ignore both claims, and what do you do instead?
Alex: First, sample size. On two hundred tasks, a ninety percent score carries a ninety-five percent confidence interval of about plus or minus four points. A two-point gap is pure noise. Second, the leaderboards probably used different harnesses, prompt scaffolding, or resource caps. Instead of trusting either, I run both models head-to-head on my own eval set using my company's specific pipeline and score them on dollar cost.
Jordan: Exactly right. Last one. An agent fixes your weekly spreadsheet correctly eighty-five percent of the time. You want to run it every weekday morning without supervision. Which metric tracks that, what does independent probability predict for the week, and why might reality diverge?
Alex: You track pass to the fifth power. If runs are independent, zero point eight five to the fifth is around forty-four percent, so it fails more than half the weeks. Reality could be worse if errors correlate, like if Friday month-end data always breaks it. Or it could be better if the failures only happen on rare edge cases you rarely encounter.
