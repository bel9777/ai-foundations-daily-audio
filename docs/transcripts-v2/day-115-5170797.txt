Alex: What kind of structure can it actually find without a teacher?
Jordan: Three main jobs. Clustering groups similar items together, like k-means. Dimensionality reduction compresses tons of variables into just a few main trends, like PCA. Alex: That brings us to self-supervised learning, which sounds like unsupervised learning, but with a twist.
Jordan: It is the engine behind modern frontier models. You hide part of the data from the model and make it guess what is missing. GPT predicts the next token. BERT fills in masked words. CLIP matches images to their web captions. Technically, there is a target and a loss function, so it looks like supervised training, but the labels are generated directly from the raw data. They cost zero dollars to make.
Alex: Which means you can train on trillions of tokens instead of paying humans to tag thousands. And that process spits out embeddings as a by-product.
Jordan: Right. To guess the next word in an invoice description, a model has to realize that diesel and off-road fuel mean roughly the same thing. That internal knowledge gets captured as vectors—lists of numbers where semantic similarity translates into geometric distance. Once everything is a vector, searching, deduplicating, and clustering become simple geometry.
Alex: You had a great analogy for this with a new staff accountant.

Alex: Labels are the steering wheel; unlabeled data is the engine. And that is exactly how we went from GPT-3 to InstructGPT. GPT-3 learned language patterns from the web without labels, and InstructGPT steered it using a tiny dataset of human instructions and ratings.

Jordan: It isolates a data point by picking random features and splitting them. If an item is totally unusual, it takes very few splits to isolate it from the herd.
