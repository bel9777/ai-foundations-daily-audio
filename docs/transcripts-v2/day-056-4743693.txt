Alex: Let's start with fast. When someone says a model is fast, they usually mean latency, right?
Jordan: Exactly. Latency is the time it takes between a user sending a request and getting a response back. If you hit enter on a chatbot and wait two seconds for the answer, that two seconds is your latency. Lower is better because nobody wants to stare at a loading spinner.
Alex: But there is a catch with how we measure latency. It is not just one number.
Jordan: Right. You have average latency, which is the typical response time across all requests. But you also have tail latency. That is the time it takes for the slowest one percent or five percent of requests. Tail latency matters because people remember the bad experiences. If ninety-nine answers arrive in one second, but the one you care about takes fifteen seconds, you will think the app is broken.
Alex: That makes sense. Now how is throughput different from latency?
Jordan: Latency is about one single request. Throughput is about the whole crowd. It is how many requests your system can handle in a given window, like requests per second or requests per minute.
Alex: Give me an analogy for that.
Jordan: Think of a restaurant kitchen. Latency is how long one customer waits at their table for a plate of pasta. Throughput is how many plates of pasta the kitchen can send out the door every hour. You could have a chef who cooks an amazing meal in five minutes flat, which is great latency. But if that chef can only cook one dish at a time, the kitchen has terrible throughput. As soon as twenty customers walk in together, people start waiting an hour.
Alex: Right, so a model might answer one prompt in half a second, but if it locks up the moment fifty people use it at once, your throughput is too low.
Jordan: Spot on. And that brings us to the third piece: cost. In machine learning serving, cost is the total pile of resources needed to keep things running. That means compute, which is the actual processing hardware doing the work, plus memory, storage, bandwidth, and the engineering time needed to maintain the servers.
Alex: And running inference, which is just using the trained model to generate an answer, takes real hardware capacity.
Jordan: It does. Big models chew through expensive GPUs. If you want ultra-low latency and massive throughput at the same time, you have to buy a ton of heavy compute capacity.
Alex: It is the classic trade-off triangle.
Jordan: It really is. Think of AI serving like a highway system. Latency is how long it takes your car to drive from point A to point B. Throughput is how many cars pass through a stretch of road every minute. Cost is the size and maintenance of the highway itself. You can build an eight-lane highway so nobody ever hits traffic, but that costs a fortune. If you build a single-lane dirt road to save money, cars pile up and everyone sits in gridlock.
Alex: Let's ground this in a real example. Say a company wants to launch a customer support chatbot.
Jordan: During the workday, thousands of users might hit the site simultaneously, so throughput has to be high. Customers also get frustrated if the bot takes ten seconds to type a sentence, so latency has to stay low. But the company cannot blow its entire budget on giant GPUs.
Alex: So what do they do?
Jordan: One common approach is routing. You run a tiny, cheap model for eighty percent of the easy questions, like checking an order status, and only route complex complaints to a larger, more expensive model. That protects your cost while keeping latency snappy for most users. But you have to measure it constantly, because if too many hard questions hit the big model at once, you will still see a bottleneck.
