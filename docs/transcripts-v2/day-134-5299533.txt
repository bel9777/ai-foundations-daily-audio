Jordan: Let us start with what a probability actually means. When a model outputs zero point eight for a prediction, that is not an absolute judgment on that single item. It is a claim about frequency.
Alex: Meaning if we gather a hundred items where the model claimed eighty percent certainty, about eighty of them should turn out to be correct.
Jordan: Exactly. Calibration is about whether accuracy matches confidence. If you group your predictions into bins, say seventy to eighty percent, eighty to ninety, and ninety to one hundred, you can plot average confidence against actual accuracy. That is a reliability diagram. On a perfectly calibrated model, those points sit right on the diagonal.
Alex: And if they sit below the diagonal, the model thinks it is smarter than it actually is. It says ninety percent, but only gets seventy percent right. That is overconfidence.
Jordan: Right. We quantify that gap with Expected Calibration Error, or ECE. It is just the average distance between confidence and accuracy across all your bins, weighted by how many samples fall into each bin.
Alex: But calibration is not accuracy, right? A terrible model could still be calibrated.
Jordan: Totally separate. A forecaster who predicts the base rate, say a twenty percent chance of rain every single day in a dry city, might be perfectly calibrated and completely useless. That is why we use proper scoring rules like log loss and the Brier score. A scoring rule is proper if you get the best score by honestly reporting your true belief. Log loss heavily penalizes confident mistakes. It forces the model to balance being accurate with being calibrated.
Alex: Do different model families behave differently here?
Jordan: They have classic failure modes. Random forests average many decision trees together, which pulls the final score toward the middle, so they rarely output zero or one. Naive Bayes goes the other way, pushing probabilities to extreme zeros and ones. And modern deep neural networks, as Guo and colleagues showed in 2017, are notorious for overconfidence.
Alex: Why overconfident?
Jordan: During training, gradient descent keeps minimizing log loss. Even after the network classifies an example correctly, it can drive the loss lower by cranking its confidence from point nine up to point nine-nine-nine. Guo's team fixed this with temperature scaling, dividing raw logits by a single learned number before softmax. For older models, people used Platt scaling or isotonic regression on a held-out set.
Alex: And once those scores actually mean what they say, we can do selective prediction.
Jordan: Exactly. You set a threshold, tau. Above tau, your loop acts automatically. Below tau, it flags the item for a human. If your confidence numbers are fake, that routing rule is just a blind guess.
Alex: This showed up clearly in the GPT-4 Technical Report, did it not?
Jordan: It did. On multiple-choice questions, the pretrained base model stayed right on the diagonal of the reliability diagram. Then post-training with RLHF wrecked that calibration. Human raters prefer confident answers, so the feedback loop rewards certainty over honesty. Kadavath and the Anthropic team saw this in 2022 as well. And an OpenAI paper from late 2025 on hallucinations pointed out that our benchmarks grade pure accuracy, so models learn to bluff rather than admit they do not know.
Alex: Plus, asking Claude or ChatGPT to rate its confidence from one to ten is just asking for generated text. That is verbalized confidence, not an actual probability.
