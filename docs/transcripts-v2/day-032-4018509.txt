Alex: AI isn't just about generating text anymore; the exact same underlying ideas can create stunning images, human-sounding speech, or even custom music tracks. This is AI Foundations, day 32.
Jordan: Today we are looking at generative AI across different modalities. Modality is just a formal word for a type of data, whether that is text, images, or audio.
Alex: So whether a model is generating a paragraph, a promotional image, or a voiceover, it is really doing the same fundamental work under the hood?
Jordan: Exactly. At a high level, every generative model learns patterns from massive amounts of training data. You can think of it as learning a compressed version of those patterns. When you give the model a prompt, it expands those patterns to generate brand-new content that follows the rules it learned.
Alex: In our recent lessons, we spent a lot of time on next-token prediction for language models. Does that same core principle apply to images and sound too?
Jordan: It really does. For text, the model predicts the next token. For audio, it predicts the next sound segment or wave pattern. For images, it might predict missing visual patches or gradually construct an image out of random static. But remember our big takeaway from earlier lessons: the model does not understand content like a human does. It is strictly doing statistical pattern matching based on its training data.
Alex: Right, no actual human brain in there. But computers only process numbers. How do you take a complex image or an audio track and turn it into something a model can actually work with?
Jordan: You break it down into manageable building blocks. Text uses tokens, which are words or pieces of words. Images get chopped into tiny square regions called image patches. Audio gets measured over tiny fractions of time called audio samples.
Alex: That sounds like a versatile chef working with different ingredients.
Jordan: That is a great mental model. The chef cooks with words for text, visual pieces for images, or sound fragments for audio. The recipe changes, but the core skill of combining learned patterns into something structured remains the same.
Alex: What about the mechanics of how these outputs get built? Is every model generating things step-by-step from start to finish?
Jordan: Not always, and that brings us to three main generation approaches. First is autoregressive generation. That is the step-by-step method common in text and some audio, where every new piece depends on what was generated before it. Second is diffusion, which is huge for images and sound. A diffusion model starts with pure random noise—like old television static—and iteratively strips away that noise in small steps until a clear image or sound emerges.
Alex: That static-clearing idea is so wild to think about. What is the third approach?
Jordan: Sequence-to-sequence, or seq2seq. That is when a model takes one ordered sequence and converts it directly into another, like turning speech audio into text, or text into spoken audio. And remember from Day 26, transformers are still the primary engine here because they excel at tracking relationships across sequences.
Alex: Let's ground this in a practical scenario. How would a real business owner actually combine these modalities?
Jordan: Imagine a small business owner launching a new product. They can prompt a text model to draft a persuasive product description. Next, they use an image model to generate a matching banner graphic for social media. Then, they use an audio model to generate a fifteen-second spoken voiceover for a local ad clip.
Alex: That sounds insanely fast, but I assume they cannot just post those outputs without looking at them first?
Jordan: Absolutely not. Generative AI is fantastic for brainstorming, drafting, and prototyping, but it still struggles with precise control, bias, and accuracy. The generated text might sound polished but hallucinate features. The image graphic might have strange visual artifacts or misplaced text. The audio clip might mispronounce the brand name. It gives you a fast first draft, but human review is non-negotiable.
Alex: Which connects right back to fine-tuning from Day 30 and human feedback from Day 31.
Jordan: Spot on. Fine-tuning adapts a general model to a specific task, domain, or brand voice using targeted data. Human feedback helps guide the model to be safer, more reliable, and more helpful. Prompts help, but human judgment is still the key to getting reliable results.
