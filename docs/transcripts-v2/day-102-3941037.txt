Alex: If you treat an AI model like a static object, you are going to be surprised when it starts failing in the real world. This is AI Foundations, day 102.
Jordan: Exactly. A lot of people think you just build a model, ship it, and you are done. But in reality, an AI is just one part of a living, breathing learning system.
Alex: So it is less of a product launch and more like maintaining a garden?
Jordan: That is a perfect way to put it. You have to keep feeding it, pruning it, and checking the soil. We call that the learning loop. It is a cycle: you collect data, clean it, train the model, evaluate it, deploy it, and monitor it. Then you use that new data to refresh the model and start the whole thing over again.
Alex: Which implies that the quality of the data is the most important part of the whole machine. If I feed my model garbage, I get garbage out, right?
Jordan: Precisely. And it is not just about having more data. It is about the right data. We usually run into three big issues: noisy data, which is just full of errors; biased data, which ignores parts of the real world; and label errors, where you are just teaching the model the wrong things. A model is only as smart as the examples you show it.
Alex: What about feedback? If I am building a chatbot and users are clicking the thumbs-up or thumbs-down buttons, does that automatically fix the model?
Jordan: Not necessarily. It is a double-edged sword. Feedback is vital because it tells you what your users actually need, but it can be really dangerous. If you only look at feedback from a tiny, vocal segment of your users, your model might start drifting to cater only to them, while ignoring everyone else. You have to sample that feedback carefully.
Alex: That sounds like it feeds into the idea of drift. I have heard that term thrown around, but is data drift the same thing as concept drift?
Jordan: They sound similar, but they are distinct. Data drift is when the input changes. Maybe your users start using a new slang term or your product names change. The task is still the same, but the language has shifted. Concept drift is deeper. It is when the relationship between input and output changes. Think of a support bot trained on an old refund policy. The user asks the same question, but the correct answer has changed because the company policy changed.
Alex: Okay, I see. So if the policy changes, I don’t just need a better model, I need better retrieval and updated documentation. It is a system issue, not just a model issue.
Jordan: You nailed it. Think of it like a kitchen. The model is the chef, but the data is the ingredients. If the ingredients are spoiled, even a Michelin-star chef cannot make a good meal. And if the menu changes—that is your concept drift—and the chef is still cooking the old recipes, your customers are going to be unhappy.
Alex: And where do humans fit in? Do we just step back and let the machine run itself?
Jordan: Rarely. We usually need a human-in-the-loop for the high-stakes stuff or when the model is unsure. Humans provide the ground truth, but we have to be careful there, too. Humans are inconsistent, and if someone tries to trick the system by feeding it bad data, they are essentially attacking the model’s brain. Your data pipeline needs as much security as your code.
Alex: I am starting to see why these systems are so hard to maintain. You can be perfect on a test set and still fail in production because the world just keeps moving.
