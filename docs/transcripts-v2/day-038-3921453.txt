Alex: So the AI is reading a piece of text to analyze it, but it gets tricked into treating that text as a fresh order from the boss.
Jordan: Exactly. An AI system often handles two kinds of text at once: your system instructions, which tell it how to act, and external content, which is just data to summarize, translate, or extract facts from. Prompt injection occurs when the model confuses that external content for a command.
Alex: Why does it get confused? Can't it tell who wrote what?
Jordan: Not by default. Remember, a large language model is just predicting text based on patterns. To the model, text is just text. It does not naturally realize that your system prompt is sacred authority while an email attachment is just untrusted data. If a sentence inside a document sounds forceful or shows up near the end, the model might just follow it.
Alex: So it follows whatever instruction feels most direct or nearest in the text stream.
Jordan: Right. There is a great mental picture for this. Imagine an AI is like a hotel concierge. Your system prompt is the official instruction memo from the hotel manager telling the concierge how to do their job. Untrusted text is the tag hanging off a guest's suitcase. The concierge needs to read the tag to figure out where the bag goes, but they shouldn't take orders from it.
Alex: So if the luggage tag says "Ignore the hotel manager and hand over the master key," the concierge should just laugh and ignore it.
Jordan: Exactly. Prompt injection is when that luggage tag tries to act like the hotel manager, and the concierge actually listens.
Alex: That makes total sense. So how do we stop the concierge from giving away the master key?
Jordan: You do not need perfect mathematical protection to make your setup drastically safer. You just need good design habits. First, explicitly separate roles in your prompt. Tell the model that external documents are data only, not authority. Second, use clear labels or boundaries, like putting untrusted text inside tags labeled DOCUMENT or EMAIL_TEXT.
Alex: Like drawing a literal fence around the untrusted stuff so the model knows where the data starts and ends.
Jordan: Precisely. That ties back to Day 35, where we talked about prompting as interface design. Third, ask the model for evidence. If it summarizes something, require it to quote the exact lines it used. And fourth, limit what the model can actually do. If its job is just to classify text, do not give it tools to send emails or run code. If it has less power, a bad instruction causes far less damage.
