Jordan: It is much more about response structure and tone. Following an instruction means format matters as much as facts. If a user says explain this like I am five, the model needs to simplify. Alex: I love analogies. What is a good way to picture this whole process?
Jordan: Imagine a master chef who knows how to cook hundreds of recipes. That is your pretrained model. The core recipes might be similar, but the service style is completely different. Fine-tuning teaches the chef the local service style. Instruction following is the chef listening when you say, make it fast, or keep it formal. The chef does not forget how to cook; they just learn how to serve.
Alex: So how does this play out in the real world? Say a company wants an AI assistant for internal HR questions.
Jordan: A general model might know broad labor laws, but it does not know company policy. It might give a vague answer or use an overly casual tone. Alex: That sounds almost too easy. What is the catch? What can go wrong when you fine-tune?
Jordan: A few big things. First is overfitting. That happens when the model memorizes the training examples so tightly that it chokes on anything slightly new. Second is catastrophic forgetting. While learning a narrow new trick, the model might accidentally lose some of its broader general capabilities.
Alex: And I assume garbage in, garbage out applies here too?
Jordan: Absolutely. Poorly written training examples teach bad habits. In fact, a small set of fifty clean, perfectly written examples will beat ten thousand messy, inconsistent ones every single time.
Alex: Makes sense. Now, before someone runs off to fine-tune a model, when do you actually need fine-tuning versus just writing a better prompt?
