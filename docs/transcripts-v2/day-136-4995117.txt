Jordan: Let us start with the core building block: what one dense layer actually computes. It takes an input vector, multiplies it by a weight matrix, adds a bias vector, and runs the result through an activation function. In math, that is h equals f of W x plus b.
Alex: So if your weight matrix has 384 rows, the layer is just re-describing your input using 384 new numbers. A change of coordinates.
Jordan: Exactly. But that little function, f, is where the whole thing succeeds or fails. It provides the nonlinearity. Without it, stacking layers does nothing. 
Alex: Because matrix multiplication is associative. W two times W one times x is just a new matrix times x.
Jordan: Right. Two linear layers collapse into one. Ten linear layers collapse into one. Think of an Excel workbook. Tab one takes your raw data and runs SUMPRODUCT. Tab two takes tab one and runs another SUMPRODUCT. Ten tabs of that collapse into a single SUMPRODUCT. You get zero extra modeling power.
Alex: But add a MAX with zero—a standard ReLU—and suddenly the sheet can express branching logic.
Jordan: Exactly. That Excel formula, MAX of zero comma SUMPRODUCT plus bias, is a ReLU layer. LLMs use smoother variants like SwiGLU or GeGLU, but the purpose is identical: bending the decision boundaries so the model can carve out complex patterns.
Alex: Let us make sure we keep the vocabulary straight. People often confuse activation functions with activations.
Jordan: The activation function is the rule, like ReLU or GELU. Activations are the actual output values the layer produces for a specific piece of text. When researchers say they pulled activations from layer twelve, they mean they saved those lists of numbers.
Alex: And the representation is that entire activation vector viewed as an encoding of meaning.
Jordan: Right. In a transformer, layers read from and write to a shared vector called the residual stream. Early layers handle surface details like tokens and spelling. Middle layers capture abstract context. If the model does its job, the final representation separates categories cleanly enough that a simple straight line can classify them.
Alex: Which is where linear probes come in. You train a basic logistic regression directly on one layer's activations to see if a concept is linearly readable yet.
Jordan: Yes. But do not assume each neuron corresponds to one concept. Models pack thousands of ideas into fewer neurons using superposition. Neurons are polysemantic; a single unit might fire on French grammar, medical terminology, and bridge construction. Concepts are directions across many neurons, not single cells.
Alex: That is why researchers build Sparse Autoencoders, or SAEs.
Jordan: Exactly. An SAE unpacks that tangled activation vector into a much wider, sparse list of features that are easier for humans to isolate.
Alex: That brings us to Gemma Scope 2. DeepMind released a full suite of SAEs trained across the Gemma 3 family, from 270 million up to 27 billion parameters.
Jordan: Right. Neel Nanda noted it covers every single layer. You can open Neuronpedia right now, paste text, and inspect the exact features firing in Gemma 3's residual stream. 
Alex: Though we should keep our skepticism handy. An October survey paper on SAEs showed these feature labels are auto-generated hypotheses, not absolute ground truth.
Jordan: Absolutely. Mechanistic interpretability is moving fast—MIT Technology Review even named it a top breakthrough for 2026—but reading a few isolated features does not mean we fully understand the model.
