Jordan: Generalization means the model can perform well on fresh examples it has never seen before. That is the whole point of AI. If you train a system to recognize photos of ripe tomatoes, and it only recognizes the exact twenty photos you fed it, it has not learned anything useful. It just memorized those specific pictures.
Alex: Right, so if it recognizes new tomatoes under different lighting, from weird angles, on a totally different farm, then it actually learned the concept of a tomato.

Jordan: We split our data into separate piles. Typically, you take your total pool of data and divide it into three buckets: a training set, a validation set, and a test set. A classic split might be seventy percent for training, fifteen percent for validation, and fifteen percent for the final test.
Alex: Okay, so seventy percent is what the model actually studies to learn the patterns. What are the other two sets doing?
Jordan: The validation set is what developers use while tuning the model's settings and tweaking choices during development. But the test set is locked away until the very end. If you keep peeking at the test set while building the model and changing your answers to fit it, the test stops being fair, and your scores look way better than real life.
Alex: You are basically grading your own homework at that point. But what happens when a model gets too focused on those training examples? Is there a word for that?
Jordan: Yes, we call that overfitting. Overfitting happens when a model fits its training data so closely that it learns noise, quirks, or random coincidences instead of the real underlying pattern.
Alex: Give me an example of how that goes wrong in practice.
Jordan: Picture a model trained to spot cows in photos. It learned the easiest shortcut available in the data.
Alex: It got lazy and picked up on a coincidence. So to catch that, we need good ways to grade these models, right?
Jordan: Exactly. We call those evaluation metrics. There is no single universal score for AI because different tasks need different measurements. Precision asks: when the model says yes, how often is it actually right? And recall asks: out of all the real positive cases out there, how many did the model actually catch?
