Alex: Today we are building a semantic search tool and a 2D map for our own messy notes, using nothing but standard-library Python. No pip installs at all. It shows you where real text features actually come from, instead of just hoping keyword matching saves the day. This is AI Foundations.
Jordan: Back in Lesson 16, we saw that linear models can only draw straight boundaries through whatever features you feed them. If your features are bad, your model is bad. An embedding model solves that for text. It takes a sentence and turns it into a fixed-length list of numbers, a vector. Sentences with similar meanings point in roughly the same direction in that high-dimensional space.
Alex: And we do not have to train a massive neural net on our laptops today. We just call one, grab the vectors, and see if the map it draws of our daily work makes sense. What do listeners need before they start?
Jordan: Python 3.9 or newer. That is it. For the embeddings, you pick one of two paths. Option A is an OpenAI API key using text-embedding-3-small. Fifty notes will cost you a fraction of a cent. Option B is completely free and offline: install Ollama, pull nomic-embed-text, and run it locally.
Alex: Step one is dead simple. Make a folder called embed-lab, and set your environment variable. Either export your OPENAI_API_KEY, or set EMBED_PROVIDER to ollama. Then comes step two, which is surprisingly fun: writing notes.txt.
Jordan: Aim for thirty to fifty short lines, typed out from memory. Think about four or five buckets from your actual week: invoice processing, script failures, reporting bugs, podcast notes, maybe yard work or farm chores. But the secret sauce is planting three specific test lines in there.
Alex: Right, the twins and the false friends. Explain how those work.
Jordan: Twins are two lines that mean the exact same thing but share almost no words. Like: "Gmail OAuth token expired so the mail job stopped" and "The inbox automation lost its login overnight." False friends do the opposite: they share a distinct keyword with totally different meanings. For instance: "Podcast RSS feed failed validation" and "Order more cattle feed before Friday."
Alex: Traditional keyword search fails both of those tests miserably. Next, you drop in the lab script, map_notes.py, and run it. What is the script actually doing under the hood?
Jordan: It reads your notes, checks a local JSON cache so you never pay to embed the same line twice, and calls the API. Once it gets the vectors, it normalizes them to length one. That means cosine similarity collapses into a simple dot product. Then it finds nearest neighbors, groups notes into clusters using k-means, and uses a pure Python PCA method called power iteration to project those hundreds of dimensions down to an X and Y coordinate in map.csv.
Alex: When you run it the first time, you should see the script report your note count as new, cached zero, and the dimension count: 1536 for OpenAI, or 768 for nomic. If you run it a second time, new drops to zero. That caching pattern is a lifesaver for production loops.
Jordan: Next, move your twins and false friends into the first eight lines of notes.txt and run it again. In the terminal, each twin should list the other as its nearest neighbor. And the cattle feed line should completely ignore the RSS feed line.
Alex: Then you test the command-line search. You pass in a phrase like "something broke while I was asleep" without quoting words from your actual notes. And boom, your overnight script failure notes show up right at the top. After that, open map.csv in Excel, make an XY scatter plot, color by cluster, and you have a literal visual map of your work.
Jordan: Now, where do people get tripped up on this lab?
Alex: The first one is mundane: API connection failures. A KeyError on OPENAI_API_KEY just means your shell does not have the variable set. Or if you get a connection refused on port 11434, Ollama is not running in the background.
Jordan: Exactly. The second trap is trickier: clusters forming around format instead of meaning. If every line that starts with "TODO:" or a timestamp bunches together in one cluster, the embedding model is latching onto surface style. Embeddings capture syntax and formatting alongside meaning. If that happens, strip out the prefixes and rerun.
Alex: And the third one is assuming similarity numbers mean the same thing across different models. If you write code expecting anything over 0.5 to be a match, switching from OpenAI to nomic will break your logic. Every model produces its own score distribution.
Jordan: Spot on. Time to see if you were paying attention. I have three check questions for you. First: why does the cache key hash the model name together with the text, instead of just hashing the text?
Alex: Because vectors from different models live in completely different vector spaces, and they often have different dimension counts. If you switch from nomic to OpenAI without changing the key, you end up mixing 768-dimension vectors and 1536-dimension vectors in the same cache. The math breaks. And even if two models shared the same length, comparing vectors from different models gives you garbage numbers that look totally valid. Including the model name forces a fresh embed.
Jordan: Clean answer. Second question: you run a query, and your top result has a similarity score of 0.41. Is that a real match? What should you do instead of trusting that raw score?
Alex: You cannot tell from the raw number alone. Score scales depend on the model and the task. Instead of staring at 0.41, look at the ranking and the margin between the top result and the runner-up. If the top result is 0.41 and everything else is 0.15, that is a strong signal. If you want a real threshold, calibrate it: test twenty queries where you know the ground truth, chart the scores, and pick a threshold that separates true hits from noise.
Jordan: Last one. Suppose you open map.csv in Excel, and your two twin notes are sitting far apart on opposite sides of the scatter plot. But your terminal output insisted they were each other's nearest neighbors. Which one do you trust?
Alex: Trust the terminal. The terminal is looking at the raw dot product across all 768 or 1536 dimensions. The Excel scatter plot is squashing all of that information down onto just two axes of greatest spread. When you crush high-dimensional space down to flat paper, points that are close together can easily look separated. The plot is just for intuition; the high-dimensional vector math is the source of truth.
