Alex: You can feed two AI models the exact same pile of training data, and they can end up behaving completely differently just because of how their internal wiring is set up. This is AI Foundations, day 88.
Jordan: That internal blueprint is what we call model architecture. It dictates how information flows from the second you feed in a prompt to the second the model gives you an answer.
Alex: So if the training data is the raw material, the architecture is the factory layout.
Jordan: That is a solid way to put it. Another way people describe it is the floor plan of a house. The floor plan does not pick out the couch or the paint colors, but it definitely decides whether you can host a fifty-person dinner party or if everyone is cramped in a hallway. In AI, almost everything we touch right now is a neural network, which is just layers of mathematical units connected together. The architecture determines how the model reads inputs, how it connects ideas, how big its context window can be, and what kinds of patterns it finds easy to pick up.
Alex: When engineers talk about building these things, they throw around terms like layers, parameters, and activations. Can we pin those down in simple terms?
Jordan: Think of a neural network as a long assembly line or a chain of coffee filters. A layer is just one station along that line. It takes in whatever came before it, transforms that information slightly, and passes it forward.
Alex: And the parameters?
Jordan: Those are the dials. They are numbers the model adjusts during training that decide how much weight to give to different signals. More parameters generally mean more capacity to learn complex nuances, but they also require more compute to run.
Alex: Which leaves activations.
Jordan: Activations are the actual outputs each layer spits out after processing the incoming signal. It is the message handed off from one station to the next.

Jordan: Spot on. More layers give you expressive power, but you pay for it on every single run. That brings us to why transformers took over the AI landscape a few years back. Before transformers, dealing with sequences was messy.
Alex: Sequence meaning ordered data, right? Like words in a sentence, lines of code, or time-stamped logs.
Jordan: Right, anything where the order changes the meaning. Earlier architectures processed sequences sequentially, one word after another. Transformers changed the game because of a mechanism called attention. Attention allows the model to look at an entire sequence at once and calculate which words matter most to which other words, even if they sit miles apart.
Alex: Can you give me an example of attention in action?
Jordan: Take the sentence: The trophy did not fit in the suitcase because it was too large. When you hit the word "it," what does "it" refer to?
Alex: The trophy.
Jordan: Now swap the last two words: The trophy did not fit in the suitcase because it was too small. Now what is "it"?
Alex: The suitcase.
Jordan: Humans do that effortlessly. Older sequence models struggled with that kind of distant cross-referencing. Attention lets the transformer draw a direct mathematical connection between "it" and "trophy" in the first sentence, and "it" and "suitcase" in the second. It compares multiple parts of the text to multiple other parts simultaneously. That ability to track long-range context across whole pages of text is why transformers run nearly every major language model today.
Alex: But transformers are not the only design out there. Where do older or specialized architectures still fit in?
Jordan: Different designs have different natural superpowers. Convolutional neural networks, or CNNs, are built to scan local neighborhoods of data first. For years, they dominated computer vision because if you are looking at an image, neighboring pixels tell you about edges and textures. Looking at pixel one and pixel ten thousand at the same time does not make much sense. Then you have recurrent neural networks, or RNNs, which process step-by-step. They were the standard for text before transformers, but they struggle when inputs get long because they tend to forget what happened early on.
Alex: It sounds like choosing tools for a specific kitchen.
Jordan: That is actually a great comparison. A single-burner camping stove, a standard home kitchen, and an industrial restaurant line can all cook food. The architecture has to match the workload and the physical constraints.
Alex: Let us look at a real-world scenario. Say a business wants an AI assistant for customer support. How does architecture shape that decision?

Jordan: Then you need a transformer-based setup with strong attention. And tying back to day 82, you probably pair that architecture with an external retrieval system and a memory store.
