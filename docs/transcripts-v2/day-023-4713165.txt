Alex: Ever wonder how a computer can figure out that a dog and a cat are similar, but a dog and an airplane have almost nothing in common?
Alex: This is AI Foundations, day 23.
Jordan: Computers don't actually read words or understand concepts the way humans do; they only process numbers. But the specific way we turn real-world things into numbers makes or breaks everything in modern artificial intelligence.
Alex: Up until now, we have mostly talked about models taking inputs and turning them into predictions. But today we are zooming in on a fundamental building block inside these networks called an embedding.
Jordan: An embedding is just a learned list of numbers that represents an item, whether that item is a word, a product, a user, or an image patch. The big idea here is that similar things end up with similar numbers, creating a kind of geometry of meaning.
Alex: Why can't we just keep things simple and assign each word a single label number? Like cat is 1, dog is 2, and banana is 3?
Jordan: Because single numbers force a fake order and fake distances that corrupt the math. In basic arithmetic, 3 is closer to 2 than it is to 1. If you use simple labels, the model will assume a banana is closer to a dog than a cat is, which completely ruins the logic.
Alex: Right, that connects back to what we covered on Day 20 about representations. Neural networks learn much better when the numeric representation of the input actually matches the structure of the real-world problem.
Jordan: Exactly. So instead of a single ID number, embeddings assign each item a vector.
Alex: A vector is just a list of numbers, like 0.2, minus 1.4, and 3.0.
Jordan: Right. You can think of a vector as a point in a multi-dimensional space. If two vectors are close together, the model treats the items they represent as similar. Cat and dog land near each other, king and queen end up near each other, while banana and airplane end up far apart.
Alex: When you say close together, you mean a literal mathematical distance between the points?
Jordan: Precisely. A standard choice is Euclidean distance, which is just the ordinary straight-line distance you would measure with a ruler between two points on a graph.
Alex: But who actually sets these spatial coordinates? Does an engineer sit down and manually program where every word goes?
Jordan: No, that would be impossible with millions of words and items. The embeddings are learned automatically during training.
Alex: So at the start, does every item just get a random point in space?
Jordan: Exactly. At first, a cat might be placed right next to a microwave. But every time the model makes a prediction, the loss function measures the error.
Alex: And then we use backpropagation, which we talked about on Day 21 as the method for assigning credit or blame to every part of the network for the final error.
Jordan: Right. Backpropagation tells the network how to nudge each embedding vector slightly closer to where it ought to be.
Alex: And as we learned on Day 22, doing thousands of those tiny, reliable updates without blowing up the optimization process gradually arranges all the points into a logical layout.
Jordan: To picture this, imagine a massive library containing millions of books. If every book gets a completely random shelf number, the numbers tell you nothing about what is inside. But if you map out the library so that history books cluster in one wing, science fiction in another, and math books in the back, you can navigate by topic.
Alex: An embedding space is basically that library map. The raw coordinates aren't dictionary definitions, but their relative positions organize meaning in a way the model can use.
Jordan: That is why embeddings work so well for real-world tasks like shopping recommendations. An online store might create embeddings for products and customers.
Alex: So running shoes, athletic socks, and water bottles get pulled near each other because people buy them together.
Jordan: Exactly. And customer embeddings capture habits like budget or favorite styles. If your customer vector sits near the running gear cluster, the system recommends items from that spot in space.
Alex: It isn't reading your mind; it's just finding nearby points in geometric space. What are some of the main ways developers use this?
Jordan: There are three huge ones. First, words and tokens—where a token is a full word, part of a word, or punctuation. Second, categorical items like city names, product IDs, or user profiles. Third, search and retrieval, where questions and documents are mapped into the same space to find fast matches.
Alex: That sounds way better than old-school one-hot encoding, where every item gets a giant list of mostly zeros.
Jordan: One-hot vectors are sparse, meaning mostly empty and massive. Embeddings compress that data into compact, dense vectors, saving memory while giving the model rich geometric relationships to work with.
Alex: So the big summary here is that embeddings turn abstract things into points in space where distance and direction capture useful relationships. Meaning becomes geometry.
Jordan: Spot on. Just keep in mind that even with a great geometric space, as we noted on Day 18 with model calibration, a well-structured internal representation doesn't automatically mean the model's final confidence outputs are calibrated correctly. A model can have fantastic embeddings and still give overconfident wrong answers.
