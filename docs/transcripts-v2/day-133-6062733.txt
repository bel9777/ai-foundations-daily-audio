Alex: Let's start with the basics. In lesson 16, linear models struggled with interactions unless we built them by hand. How does a single decision tree handle that?
Jordan: A decision tree is just a chain of yes-or-no questions on single features. Like, is the email sender from our tenant portal? Is the hour past five p.m.? At each node, it splits the data in two. At the bottom, in the leaves, it predicts the average value for regression, or the class frequency for classification.
Alex: How does it decide where to cut?
Jordan: It is greedy. At every node, it scans every feature and every threshold, keeping the split that reduces impurity the most. That is squared error for regression, and Gini or entropy for classification. Because a split down one branch only applies to rows in that branch, feature interactions happen automatically.
Alex: And you do not need feature scaling. But what is the trade-off?
Jordan: Two big ones. First, leaf predictions are flat averages, so trees cannot extrapolate. If a house price is higher than anything in training, the tree just predicts its highest leaf value and flatlines. Second, variance. Let a tree grow unchecked, and it splits until every row has its own leaf. Zero training error, but it memorized the noise. Low bias, huge variance.
Alex: That is where ensembles come in. You had a great property appraisal analogy for this.
Jordan: Picture appraising a building. One appraiser studying a single batch of comps is a deep tree. They are confident, but wrong in their own specific way. Bagging, or a random forest, is hiring a panel of five hundred appraisers. You give each appraiser a bootstrap sample—a random subset of rows drawn with replacement—and at every split, they can only consider a random subset of features. That decorrelates them. When you average their estimates, their individual errors cancel out.
Alex: And about thirty-seven percent of the rows are left out of each tree, giving you out-of-bag error for free. How is boosting different from the panel?
Jordan: Boosting is an appraisal review chain. Instead of building trees in parallel, you build them sequentially. Reviewer one gives a rough estimate: the dataset average. Reviewer two looks strictly at where reviewer one was wrong—the residual error—and fits a shallow tree to that. You multiply that correction by a learning rate, add it to the running prediction, and repeat.
Alex: So random forests cut variance, while boosting cuts bias. And this pattern is all over frontier AI, right?
Jordan: Absolutely. Taking a majority vote across multiple LLM answers in self-consistency prompting is bagging. Residual connections in deep neural networks work just like boosting, adding corrections step by step. Just do not confuse this with mixture-of-experts. An MoE layer routes tokens to specific experts; it does not average full models.
Alex: Walk me through what XGBoost actually calculates in one boosting round.
Jordan: Take four buildings with maintenance costs: ten and twelve thousand dollars for two buildings under age forty, thirty and thirty-four thousand for two older ones. The starting prediction is the mean: twenty-one point five. The residuals are negative eleven point five, negative nine point five, plus eight point five, and plus twelve point five. Total squared error is four fifty-one. XGBoost splits at age forty.
Alex: How does it calculate the leaf values?
Jordan: The formula for a leaf weight is negative sum of gradients divided by the sum of Hessians plus lambda, where lambda is L2 regularization, default one. For squared error, the Hessian is just one per row. For the young buildings, the sum of gradients is twenty-one, and the sum of Hessians is two. Twenty-one divided by two plus one gives seven. So the leaf adjustment is negative seven.
Alex: If we just averaged raw residuals, we would have subtracted ten point five, but lambda shrunk it to seven.
Jordan: Exactly. The older leaf gets plus seven. Then we multiply by our learning rate, eta, which defaults to zero point three. That nudges predictions by two point one: down to nineteen point four for young buildings, up to twenty-three point six for older ones. Squared error drops from four fifty-one to two ninety-seven. Tree two then fits whatever error is left. And in XGBoost 3.3, categorical columns work natively without manual one-hot encoding.

Jordan: Question one. Your unlimited-depth tree for Gmail triage hits ninety-nine percent training accuracy, but drops to seventy-one percent on five-fold cross-validation. A random forest gets eighty percent. Explain that gap, and give me two tweaks you would test before picking a model.
Alex: The gap is variance. The single tree memorized noise in specific emails. The random forest averaged decorrelated trees, washing that noise out. For tweaks: first, constrain the single tree with max depth or minimum leaf size inside cross-validation. Second, try gradient boosting with early stopping. And I would verify whether accuracy even makes sense here—if replies are rare, log loss against a dummy baseline is much safer.
Jordan: Question two. You train boosted trees on 2019 to 2025 farm input prices to forecast 2027 fertilizer costs. Actual prices climb above anything in your training window, but your forecast stays completely flat. Why, and how do you fix it?
Alex: Trees are piecewise constant. They cannot extrapolate beyond the maximum value seen during training. To fix it, do not predict raw price levels. Predict year-over-year percentage changes or ratios instead, which stay within historical ranges, then compound forward. Or fit a linear model to capture the upward trend, and use boosting on its residuals.
