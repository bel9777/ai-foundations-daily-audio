Alex: If an artificial intelligence can pass professional exams in seconds, why does it trip over its own feet the moment you ask it to manage a simple multi-step project? This is AI Foundations, day 97.
Jordan: We see that confusion all the time, and it usually comes down to mixing up reasoning and planning. They sound like the same thing, but in AI, they play very different roles. Reasoning means using information to reach a conclusion. The system takes facts, rules, or patterns and connects them to answer a question or pick an option.
Alex: Like figuring out that your car won't start because the battery terminals are corroded.
Jordan: Exactly. That is reasoning. Planning, on the other hand, means deciding on an ordered sequence of steps to reach a goal. It is not the goal itself. It is the roadmap from where you are right now to where you want to be.
Alex: So an AI could be brilliant at explaining why something happened, but totally fall apart when you ask it to lay out an airtight four-week project schedule.
Jordan: Precisely. A model might generate an answer that sounds completely convincing, but if you look at the plan underneath, it might fall apart the moment anything in the real world shifts. To build systems that do not break like that, you have to break planning down into three simple building blocks: the goal, the state, and the action.
Alex: Break those down for us.
Jordan: The goal is the outcome you want. Say, finishing a client presentation by Friday afternoon. The state is the current reality. Right now, your state might be that you have three bullet points, a messy spreadsheet, and zero slides built. An action is a single move you can make to alter that state, like outlining the introduction or emailing a coworker for missing revenue figures. Good planning just means looking at the state, looking at the goal, and asking what should happen next.
Alex: But in real life, there is almost never just one straight line from state to goal.
Jordan: That is where search comes in. In AI, search means exploring several possible sequences of moves before you lock one in. Think of packing for a trip. The goal is your destination, the state is your empty suitcase and the weather forecast, and the plan is what you pack and the order you pack it. If you pack without checking the forecast, you might have a neat suitcase, but you freeze when you land in a snowstorm. Search allows the system to weigh different paths instead of grabbing the very first idea that pops into its head.
Alex: And if you are packing for a month-long trek across five climates, you cannot do it all in one mental leap. You have to break the problem apart.
Jordan: That is decomposition. You take a big, intimidating problem and split it into smaller, manageable chunks. Think about launching an email newsletter. If you tell an AI to just go launch it, you will get a messy, generalized mess. But if you decompose it, you get discrete milestones: identify the target audience, create the design template, draft the first article, test the signup link, and finally send it out and track feedback.
Alex: And each of those smaller steps is vastly easier to check.
Jordan: That brings us to verification. A step is completely useless unless it actually moves you closer to the goal without breaking something along the way. This is where all our recent work ties together. Verification connects directly to evaluation, because we have to judge a plan by measurable results, not by how confident the model sounds. It connects to reliability, because a plan fails if the system is brittle or inconsistent. And it connects to safety, because a plan might be technically effective while doing something risky or unauthorized.
Alex: So a solid planner cannot just be an idea generator. It has to act like an inspector.
Jordan: Always. Look at a small online store dealing with hundreds of repetitive shipping questions. If you ask a raw language model how to handle customer support emails, it might suggest automating everything with chatbots. That is reckless. A reasoned plan identifies the goal, which is faster replies and less human burnout. It maps the state: incoming emails with a small team that only has two hours a day. Then it chooses actions: categorize frequent questions, build clear templates, route high-stakes complaints directly to a human, and verify that outgoing drafts are accurate before sending.
Alex: The verification there is measuring whether response times dropped and whether customer complaints decreased. It solves the actual operational problem instead of just throwing tech at the wall.
Jordan: Exactly. And that distinction matters because advanced models stumble into four predictable traps when planning on their own. The first is shortcutting, where the model leaps to a conclusion without checking options. The second is overplanning, where it builds a ridiculously complex thirty-step plan that sounds genius but immediately collapses in practice. The third is state blindness, where the model forgets what it already did and loops on the same step. And the fourth is weak verification, where it assumes an action succeeded simply because the sentence describing it sounded professional.
Alex: So how do we protect our workflows from those four traps?
Jordan: Two practical habits. First, keep plans short enough to check. Long plans hide compounding errors. Second, separate decide from do. First, have the model generate the proposed plan. Review and inspect that plan. Then, execute the steps. Never let a system run wild executing a multi-step sequence without that separation.
Alex: That keeps a human in the loop where it counts.
Jordan: Exactly. Now, let us test this. Time for your knowledge check. Question one: in your own words, what is the difference between reasoning and planning?
Alex: Reasoning is using facts and logic to draw a conclusion or understand a situation. Planning is mapping out an ordered sequence of actions to reach a specific future goal.
Jordan: Right on the money. Question two: what are the three foundational building blocks of planning?
Alex: Goal, state, and action. The goal is what you want to achieve, the state is where things stand right now, and the action is the specific step you take to change that state.
Jordan: Clean and simple. Third question: why is verification so crucial during the planning process?
Alex: Because a plan can sound polished and convincing while leading you straight into a wall. Verification confirms that each action actually succeeded and made genuine progress toward the goal before you move to the next step.
