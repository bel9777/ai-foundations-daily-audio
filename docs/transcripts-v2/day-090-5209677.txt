Alex: If you want an AI to give reliable answers about your private files or today's news without making things up, you have to give it a way to look things up. This is AI Foundations, day 90.
Jordan: Exactly. We know models are great at generating text from patterns they learned during training. But training ends. The model does not know your personal notes, your company policies, or what happened five minutes ago. That is why we need retrieval.
Alex: Which basically means finding outside information and handing it to the model right when it needs it.
Jordan: Exactly. Think of the model as a brilliant student who has read thousands of textbooks. They know how to write and think, but they still need to check the library for the newest product manual before answering a specific question. Retrieval is that library run.
Alex: And doing that run solves three big problems right away. First is freshness, because you get live information. Jordan: That brings us to memory. People use the word memory loosely with AI, but in system design, we break it into three specific types. Short-term memory is what the system holds during a single conversation or task. That is usually just the text sitting inside the model's active context window right now.
Alex: The context window being the hard limit on how many words the model can see at one time.
Jordan: Right. Then you have working memory, which is the immediate slice of information the model is actively juggling to reason through a step. And finally, long-term memory is what you save across sessions, like user preferences, past project notes, or ongoing summaries. But here is the catch: good memory is not hoarding. You cannot just save every single character forever.
Alex: It has to be selective. Store what matters, discard the noise, and be able to pull it back up later. How does an AI actually pull that information up? What does the pipeline look like?
Jordan: A standard retrieval pipeline has three steps: search, select, and insert. First, you search through your pool of documents or notes. Second, you select the most relevant chunks. Third, you insert those chunks directly into the prompt so the model can read them and answer.
Alex: You said chunks. Why not just hand over the whole document?
Jordan: Because a fifty-page document will eat up your context window and drown the model in irrelevant text. So we break documents down into chunks, usually paragraphs or short sections. That way, the system grabs only the paragraph that directly answers the question.
Alex: And how does it know which paragraph matches? It is not just simple keyword search, right?
Jordan: Usually, no. Modern systems use embeddings. An embedding converts a piece of text into a list of numbers that capture its meaning. If two sentences mean roughly the same thing, their embeddings sit close to each other in mathematical space. A vector database is built specifically to store those embeddings and find matching meanings instantly. It is like a smart filing cabinet indexed by concept rather than alphabetical order.
Alex: That handles search. What about deciding how to store things in memory in the first place?
Jordan: You generally have three choices. First, store exact facts when precision is non-negotiable, like a customer's account number, a project deadline, or legal terms. Second, store summaries when you only care about the gist, which saves space. Third, store references or pointers when a file is huge and you only want to fetch it on demand.
Alex: Summaries save space, but you inevitably lose small details.
Jordan: That is why well-designed systems often store both a summary for quick skimming and a direct reference to the raw source. This ties back to what we covered on Day 84 with evaluation. You cannot just check if the model's final answer sounds confident. You have to evaluate the retrieval step. Did the system actually pull the right chunk, or did it grab the wrong evidence and wing it?
Alex: That reminds me of our customer support example. Say a customer asks, what is your return policy for opened items?
Jordan: Perfect example. If the model guesses based on general internet trends, it might tell the customer yes when the company policy says no. Instead, the system searches the company policy database, retrieves the exact paragraph on opened goods, inserts it into the prompt, and writes an answer strictly from that text.
Alex: And if the company changes the policy next week, you do not need to retrain a massive model. You just edit that single paragraph in your document store.
Jordan: Exactly. Plus, tying back to Day 85 on safety, restricting retrieval to verified, internal documents prevents the model from pulling in rogue, outdated, or unsafe instructions.

They keep the counter clear, grab what they need for that specific dish, and cook.
