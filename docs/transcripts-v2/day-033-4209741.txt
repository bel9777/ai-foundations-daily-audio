Jordan: Because when a language model generates text, it almost never has just one single word in mind. At each step, it calculates probability scores for thousands of possible next tokens. Sampling is the choice process where the model selects a token from a bucket of likely options instead of blindly picking the single top-ranked choice every time.
Alex: So if it always picked the absolute top token, it would be totally predictable.
Jordan: Predictable, but also repetitive and dry. Sampling allows the model to choose among several sensible options, giving the output natural variety and flair.
Alex: Okay, but how does it decide how adventurous or wild to be with those picks? That brings us to temperature, right?
Jordan: Exactly. Temperature is a setting that controls how focused or spread out those choices are during sampling. Think of it as a thermostat for creativity. A colder setting narrows the choices down to the safest, most likely tokens. A warmer setting widens the bucket to include less common tokens.
Alex: Does a higher temperature make the model smarter or dumber?
Jordan: Neither. Temperature does not change the model's factual knowledge or underlying intelligence at all. It simply adjusts how much randomness is permitted in the output. And in this context, randomness is not chaos. It is controlled variation among plausible options.
Alex: You shared a great mental model for this earlier using a restaurant menu. Walk us through that chef analogy again.
Jordan: Imagine a restaurant with a huge menu, and the chef has to choose a dish for you based on your order. Sampling is the chef choosing the meal from many options. Temperature is how adventurous the chef feels today. At a low temperature, the chef picks the absolute safest, most standard dish that matches your request. At a higher temperature, the chef might choose a less obvious, more creative dish that still fits what you asked for.
Alex: But if the chef gets too adventurous, you might end up with something weird that you did not want at all.
Jordan: Exactly. That tradeoff is output diversity, which is how different the answers are across multiple runs. High diversity gives you fresh ideas, but if diversity is too high, the answers start drifting away from your original prompt.
Alex: Give me a concrete example. How does this actually look in real life?
Jordan: Say you ask a model for a product slogan for a water bottle. At a low temperature, it plays it safe and gives you something simple like, Drink better. Carry lighter.

Alex: So it is not about setting temperature high or low permanently. It is a task choice.
