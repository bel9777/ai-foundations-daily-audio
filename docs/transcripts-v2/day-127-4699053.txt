Alex: When all the frontier models cluster within a point or two of 95 percent, a tiny gap on a few hundred questions is statistically meaningless.
Jordan: That's why public leaderboards can't make your decisions. You have to run your own evals.
Alex: But if you evaluate your tweaks carelessly, you'll end up shipping changes that are just random flukes. This is AI Foundations, lesson 14.
Jordan: Start with the basic holdout set. You evaluate a model on, say, 200 emails it never saw, and it scores 80 percent accuracy. Most people write down 80 percent and move on.
Alex: But 200 emails is just a small sample, which means variance.
Jordan: Right. Standard error for accuracy is roughly the square root of p times one minus p over n. At 80 percent across 200 items, your 95 percent confidence interval is roughly plus or minus five and a half points. Another batch could easily score 75 or 85 percent.
Alex: Which is why we use k-fold cross-validation. You split the data into k parts, train on k minus one, test on the remaining fold, and rotate so every item gets tested once.
Jordan: Exactly. You report the mean and the spread across folds. If classes are imbalanced, like invoices making up only eight percent of your inbox, StratifiedKFold preserves that exact ratio in every single fold.
Alex: But cross-validation breaks down if your split doesn't match how the model sees the real world.
Jordan: It breaks in two big ways. Group leakage happens when the same tenant or vendor lands in both train and test folds, so the model memorizes identities instead of learning patterns. Temporal leakage happens when you train on October to predict September. Use GroupKFold to keep entities intact within a single fold, and TimeSeriesSplit to only train on the past to predict the future.
Alex: It's like an agronomist testing seed varieties. You don't plant variety A on rich soil in spring and variety B on rocky soil in autumn. You plant them side by side in the same plot so soil differences cancel out. That's paired testing. You repeat across plots like folds, and across seasons like time splits.
Jordan: And you never evaluate final yield on the plot where you dialed in your fertilizer schedule. If you test 40 prompt variants and take the highest cross-validation score, that top score is inflated by the winner's curse. Some of that edge was just luck.
Alex: To get an honest number, you need an untouched test set you only touch once, or nested cross-validation where an inner loop selects and an outer loop scores.
Jordan: Anthropic highlighted this in late 2024 when Evan Miller published their framework on adding error bars to evals. His core message was to treat eval questions like a survey sample, complete with standard errors and cluster-aware adjustments.
Alex: And always use paired comparisons when testing two models on the same data.
Jordan: Always. Say Prompt A gets 122 of 150 emails right, which is 81.3 percent, and Prompt B gets 127 right, or 84.7 percent. Naively, B looks three points ahead, but their standard error intervals overlap heavily.
Alex: When you look example by example, both prompts agree on 131 emails.
Jordan: They only disagree on 19. B got 12 right where A failed, and A got 7 right where B failed. On a fair coin flip, a 12-to-7 split or worse happens 36 percent of the time. There's zero evidence B is actually better.

Jordan: First scenario. You predict late-paying tenants using 36 months of data, with one row per tenant per month. A random 5-fold cross-validation gives you an AUC of 0.91, but live performance completely collapses. What leaked, and how do you fix the setup?
Alex: Two leaks. The same tenants appeared in both train and test folds, letting the model memorize people instead of risk patterns. And random splits let it train on future months to predict past ones. Fix it with TimeSeriesSplit to train strictly on earlier months, and group by tenant so you evaluate on tenants the model has never seen.
Jordan: Right on both counts. Second: your script checker flags factual errors. Prompt B catches 9 errors A missed, and A catches 4 errors B missed, across 120 scripts. But 10 scripts belong to the same multi-part episode. Do you switch to B?
