Alex: If you want an AI to answer questions about your company's latest policies without making things up, you need a way to feed it the facts at the exact moment it needs them. This is AI Foundations, day 39. Yesterday we talked about prompt injection and untrusted text. Today we build on that by asking a practical question: how do we give a model useful information without forcing it to remember everything from its training? The answer is Retrieval-Augmented Generation, or RAG. Jordan, start us off with what that mouthful actually means.
Jordan: It breaks down into three simple words. First, retrieval: searching for specific, useful information from a set of documents, web pages, or database records. Second, context: the actual text the model can see right now while writing its answer. Third, generation: the model writing a natural language response based on that context.
Alex: So instead of asking the model to rely purely on what it learned in the past, we hand it a small packet of evidence first.
Jordan: Exactly. And when an answer relies on that provided evidence rather than just the model's memory, we say the answer is grounded.
Alex: Why not just let the model answer straight from its memory?
Jordan: Because large language models are great at language, but terrible at acting like reliable databases. Their training data gets outdated, it doesn't know your private internal documents, and as we covered back on day 34, models hallucinate. They give super confident answers that sound polished but are totally false. RAG helps reduce that risk by supplying current or domain-specific facts right when the answer is being generated.
Alex: That makes sense for things like company policies, product details, or schedules that change all the time. Plus you get traceability, so you actually know where the answer came from.
Jordan: Spot on. Think of it like an open-book exam. Without RAG, the model takes a closed-book test relying strictly on memory. With RAG, we let it open the textbook to the exact right page before answering.
Alex: But having the book open doesn't guarantee you get an A.
Jordan: Right. If the retrieved text is wrong, incomplete, or badly chosen, the model can still give a bad answer. RAG isn't a magic truth machine. It just gives the model a much better shot at being accurate.
Alex: Walk us through the basic RAG pipeline. How does it work step by step?
Jordan: There are four core steps. First, store information in a searchable format. Second, when a user asks a question, retrieve the most relevant pieces of text. Third, insert those retrieved passages directly into the prompt context. Fourth, generate an answer using both the question and that evidence.
Alex: You said passages instead of whole documents there.
Jordan: That is an important distinction. A document is any full source text, like a policy manual or meeting note. A passage is a smaller piece of that document, usually a paragraph or short section. Retrieval is about picking the exact right pages from the library, not dragging the whole library into the chat.
Alex: How does this tie back to the prompt engineering strategies we've learned this week?
Jordan: RAG doesn't replace good prompting; it relies on it. From day 36, we know clear instructions, context, and constraints matter. A good RAG prompt explicitly tells the model how to treat the retrieved text. For example, "Use only the provided sources," or "If the sources do not contain the answer, say you do not know."
Alex: And from day 37, we can use structured outputs, like asking the model to break its response into an answer, supporting evidence, and uncertainty score.
Jordan: Exactly. And don't forget day 38: because retrieved text might come from untrusted documents, the system should treat that text strictly as data to read, not as commands to follow.
Alex: Let me make sure I've got the real-world picture. Say a company has an internal policy manual covering vacation rules, expense limits, and onboarding.
Jordan: An employee asks, "Can I expense a meal over fifty dollars?" The RAG system searches the manual, pulls back the specific policy passage on meal limits, and places it into the prompt context. Then the model writes a clear response using only that snippet.
Alex: That is way better than hoping the model memorized the company rulebook correctly, and way better than forcing employees to read a fifty-page document every time they have a question.
Jordan: Exactly. And let's be clear on what RAG is not. It is not retraining or fine-tuning a model on your files. It is not a guarantee of absolute truth. And it isn't just pasting an entire massive file into a prompt. The core idea is selective context—bringing in only the most relevant information at the exact moment it's needed.
Jordan: Let me quiz you on today's concepts. Question one: In one sentence, what does RAG do?
Alex: RAG first retrieves relevant information from a source, then passes that text to the model as context so it can generate a grounded answer.
Jordan: Exactly. Question two: Why can a RAG system still fail even if it retrieves relevant text?
Alex: It can fail if the source text itself is wrong or incomplete, or if the model misreads the passage, ignores the evidence, and fills in gaps with guesses.
Jordan: Perfect sharpness. Question three: What is one instruction you might include in a prompt to keep a RAG answer grounded?
Alex: You could tell it "Use only the provided sources," or "If the answer is not in the text, state that you do not know," or even ask it to cite the exact passage.
