Alex: So Jordan, I was reading this revised paper that hit arXiv on September 22nd, 2026. It is called MONA: Muon Optimizer with Nesterov Acceleration, by Li and team.
Jordan: Right, the v2 update. The original dropped back in May.
Alex: They are trying to upgrade Muon, which is wild considering AdamW has been the untouchable standard for almost a decade.
Jordan: It really has. Muon started out in late 2024 as this clever speedrun optimizer that orthogonalized weight updates instead of just tracking coordinate momentum. Then Moonshot AI actually used it to train Kimi K2 at frontier scale in 2025. Now the MONA preprint claims Muon can get stuck in sharp valleys, so they added Nesterov momentum to help it escape.
Alex: But we should keep our guard up, right? It is still an unverified preprint.
Jordan: Absolutely. It has not shipped in a confirmed production model, and nobody has independently replicated the speedups yet. But it shows where the battle lines are drawn.
Alex: When you stack dozens of layers in a neural network, backpropagation turns into an exponential runaway train, or it completely stalls out. This is AI Foundations, lesson 22.
Jordan: And that breakdown happens whether you are running an unattended Python script on your laptop overnight, or spending millions on a cluster.
Alex: You always use an irrigation analogy for this.
Jordan: Think of a deep network as a long irrigation pipe with dozens of joints. You need water to reach the furthest field at a steady pressure. If pressure doubles at every joint, the pipe bursts. That is your exploding gradient. If pressure drops by half at every joint, the far end gets a dry trickle. That is vanishing gradients.
Alex: Because every layer multiplies the signal.
Jordan: Exactly. Say a layer scales variance by a factor k. Over twenty layers, that multiplier is k to the twentieth power. If k is 1.5, you end up at over thirty-three hundred times the signal. If k is 0.7, you are down to less than a thousandth.
Alex: So how do we fix the plumbing?
Jordan: Fix one is initialization. Before training starts, you size the pipes so k is roughly one. For ReLU networks, you use He initialization, scaling weights by the square root of two divided by the number of inputs.
Alex: But as soon as training starts, weights move and the pipe sizes drift.
Jordan: That is where fix two comes in: normalization and residual connections. Residual connections let the gradient bypass layers entirely through an addition step, x plus f of x. That is an express water main. LayerNorm or RMSNorm acts as a pressure regulator at every single joint. Today, we put that norm before the layer, called pre-LN, which keeps things far more stable.
Alex: And fix three is how we actually turn on the water.
Jordan: The optimizer and schedule. AdamW handles each parameter individually, while Muon treats whole weight matrices. But whichever you use, you warm up the learning rate slowly so noisy early gradients do not break things, and then decay it toward the end.
Alex: Which leaves fix four for emergencies: gradient clipping and rollback.
Jordan: The pressure-relief valve. If the gradient norm spikes past a threshold on some weird batch, clipping rescales it down before it ruins the model weights. And if training still blows up into NaNs, you restore your last checkpoint.
Alex: That difference between firefighting and prevention showed up vividly when you compare Meta's OPT-175B in 2022 to Kimi K2 in 2025.
Jordan: Meta was refreshingly honest in their logbook. OPT had repeated loss spikes. The engineers literally had to watch logs, stop runs, roll back to an earlier checkpoint, and restart with a lower learning rate. Pure manual firefighting.
Alex: And Kimi K2 did not have to do that?
Jordan: Moonshot noticed that their attention logits were blowing up during Muon training. Instead of waiting for the global gradient to explode and rolling back, they built MuonClip. It detects when query and key activations spike and rescales those specific weight matrices immediately. They claimed 15.5 trillion tokens without a single loss spike.
Alex: Targeted prevention at the source rather than mopping up the floor after the pipe bursts. Time to see if I can diagnose these plumbing failures myself. Hit me with the quiz.
Jordan: Scenario one. You are fine-tuning a small classifier on your emails overnight. For thirty-nine steps, loss drops nicely and the gradient norm is steady at three. Step forty hits: loss is NaN, gradient norm is four thousand. What are your first three moves, and what do you leave alone?
Alex: First move, I do not touch the model architecture or change the optimizer. Thirty-nine healthy steps mean the setup works. My first action is inspecting the exact batch of emails from step forty. It is probably one corrupt, giant, or empty email that caused the spike. Second, I discard the NaN weights and roll back to the step thirty-nine checkpoint. Third, I turn on gradient clipping with a threshold around five, and maybe add a small learning rate warmup if it keeps happening.
Jordan: Spot on. That is the exact playbook OPT used. Scenario two. A colleague shows you a twenty-layer network with no residual connections and no normalization. Training loss has sat completely flat at the baseline mean since step zero. The gradient norm at the output layer is 0.5, but at layer one, it is ten to the negative eighth. Diagnose it, and give me two fixes ranked by code effort.
Alex: That is vanishing gradients. The early layers are basically getting zero updates, so the network can only learn to predict the average. Cheapest fix is one line of code: switch the weights to He initialization. The second fix takes a bit more work: add residual skip connections and insert RMSNorm or LayerNorm before each block.
