Alex: Anthropic posted a paper on X a few weeks ago about using Claude to police Claude. They call it self-assessment, and it has been running in production since June.
Jordan: Right. Right after Claude responds, it looks at the whole exchange and scores whether something went off the rails. On Claude Fable 5, it catches 94 percent of high-priority harmful conversations with only a 1 percent false-positive rate.
Alex: That headline sounds incredible until you read down to the blocking benchmarks. When actual attackers try to push past it, attack success only drops from 79 percent down to 54 percent. Meanwhile, a dedicated prompted classifier knocked attacks down to 25 percent.
Jordan: Exactly. Anthropic was totally upfront about it: as an automated blocker, self-assessment is weak. But as an offline flag that queues conversations for human safety review, it is fast and cheap. The lesson is that the exact same detector can be great or terrible depending entirely on the job you hand it. Also, keep in mind their benchmark ground truth was another stronger model, not human labelers.
Alex: Every single yes-or-no automation you build makes a hidden bet on which kind of mistake you can afford. This is AI Foundations, lesson 13.
Jordan: Most automations live on a binary choice. Is this email an invoice? Does this customer ticket need an urgent escalation? People usually check how often the model gets it right and quote an accuracy score like 95 percent. But accuracy is a trap.
Alex: Because accuracy treats all mistakes as equal.
Jordan: And it hides what is actually happening. We split reality into a confusion matrix with four buckets. True positives, where you flag something and you are right. True negatives, where you leave it alone and it was fine. False positives, which are false alarms. And false negatives, which are outright misses. If you run a scam detector where only one in a hundred emails is fraud, a dumb model that does nothing at all gets 99 percent accuracy while missing every single criminal.
Alex: So we throw out raw accuracy and look at the rates inside the matrix. Recall, precision, and false-positive rate.
Jordan: Yes. Recall is: of the real positives out there, how many did you catch? False-positive rate is: of all the innocent items, what percentage did you wrongly accuse? Those two are fixed properties of your detector on a given dataset. But precision—which is the percentage of your alarms that are actually real—depends heavily on your base rate.
Alex: Walk through the math on that, because that is where people get blindsided.
Jordan: Take a Gmail triage loop processing 2,000 emails a month. Say only 40 are invoices. That is a two percent base rate. Suppose you build a classifier with 95 percent recall and a tiny 1 percent false-positive rate. Sounds bulletproof, right?
Alex: Sounds ready for production.
Jordan: You will catch 38 of the 40 invoices. But that 1 percent false-positive rate hits the 1,960 non-invoices. That gives you roughly 20 false alarms. Now look at your inbox. You have 38 real invoices and 20 junk flags. Your precision is 38 divided by 58, which is about 66 percent. One out of every three flagged emails is a false alarm, even with a pristine 1 percent false-positive rate. If invoices were rarer, false alarms would completely bury you.
Alex: And you do not fix that by getting a new model. You fix it by moving the decision threshold.
Jordan: Right. The model does not output yes or no. It outputs a score, like a probability from zero to one hundred. You choose the cutoff. If you lower the cutoff, recall goes up because you catch more things, but precision drops because you trigger more false alarms.
Alex: It is like a smoke detector with a sensitivity dial.
Jordan: Exactly. If you install it in your kitchen, you dial the sensitivity down so making toast does not wake the whole street. That is tolerating misses to avoid costly false alarms. Put that same detector in a hay barn, and you crank the sensitivity to maximum. A false alarm costs you a short walk across the yard. A miss burns down the farm.
Alex: The cost of the mistake dictates the dial.
Jordan: That is the whole ballgame. If a false positive costs C-F-P and a miss costs C-F-N, and your model gives honest probabilities, you pull the trigger whenever probability exceeds C-F-P divided by the sum of both costs. When misses are catastrophic, your threshold should sit way down near 5 or 10 percent.
Alex: We saw Anthropic wrestling with this exact trade-off in their Constitutional Classifiers work.
Jordan: Yes. In their early 2025 release, their safety checks drove a 0.38 percent increase in refusals on harmless user queries. That sounds tiny, but on millions of chats, thousands of paying users were getting unfairly blocked. By their 2026 follow-up, they standardized their benchmark: lock false-positive rate at exactly 0.1 percent on general traffic, and then see who gets the highest recall.
Alex: But even that benchmark had a blind spot. On general chat, the false-positive rate was 0.1 percent, but on chemistry questions, it shot up to 1.5 percent.
Jordan: An order of magnitude worse. If you are a chemist or a defensive security researcher, the classifier feels broken. We saw security engineers filing bug reports on Claude Code and OpenAI forums in late 2026 because defensive code was getting flagged constantly. The system-wide false-alarm rate was low, but their specific corner of traffic suffered high false alarms.
Alex: Alright, let us test this. Quiz me.
Jordan: Scenario one. Your accounts-payable fraud check scans 5,000 vendor emails a month. Half a percent are fraudulent payment changes. Your detector has 96 percent recall and a 2 percent false-positive rate. How many flags will you review, what share are real, and how would you fix it?
Alex: Half a percent of 5,000 is 25 actual frauds. With 96 percent recall, you catch 24. But 2 percent of the 4,975 clean emails is about 100 false alarms. So you review 124 flags, and only 24 are real. That is roughly 19 percent precision. Four out of five alerts are fake. To fix it, you do not just raise the threshold and miss fraud. You add a cheap heuristic rule first, like only scanning emails with bank detail changes from new domains, to shrink the negative pool before running the model.
Jordan: Spot on. Scenario two: our invoice detector. A missed invoice costs 40 dollars in late fees and manual cleanup. A false flag costs three dollars of your time to review. The model outputs calibrated probabilities. Where does the threshold go, and what do you verify first?
Alex: Three divided by three plus 40 is three over 43, roughly 0.07. You flag anything with a 7 percent score or higher. It is super aggressive because a miss costs 13 times more than a false alarm. But before trusting that, I would check model calibration. I need to know that when the model says seven percent, items in that bucket actually turn out positive seven percent of the time.
Jordan: Scenario three. Anthropic reported self-assessment caught 94 percent of high-priority misuse at 1 percent false-positive rate, but only cut attack success from 79 percent to 54 percent as a blocker. Why both, and where would you use self-assessment in your own automated Claude Code loops?
Alex: Both are true because the first is passive detection on normal conversations against another model, while the second faces an adversary actively crafting inputs to fool the detector. In my own Claude Code workflows, I would use an LLM self-check to flag logs or queue tickets for human review. I would never let it act as a standalone gatekeeper for irreversible actions like wiping databases or transferring money.
