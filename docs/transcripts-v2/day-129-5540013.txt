Alex: An Emergent Mind research summary on linear probes caught my eye this week. It highlights how frontier labs are using basic linear classifiers to monitor the inner thoughts of massive language models.
Jordan: Right. They updated that summary in July 2026, pulling together work from Peng and others. The idea is straightforward: you freeze the model's residual stream—that running internal state passing between transformer layers—and train a simple logistic regression probe on top of it.
Alex: And Peng pointed out that these probes aren't summarizing what a whole concept looks like. They're just finding discriminative directions in that frozen feature space. Other cited work, like CLP-DD, even showed you can solve these probes in closed form without tuning a learning rate at all.
Jordan: The wild part is that monitoring frontier models often comes down to the exact math people use in introductory statistics. But that also means these probes inherit every single limitation of linear models.
Alex: If you understand what a linear model can and can't represent, you can spot the bugs in your own spam filter and know when frontier safety claims are overhyped. This is AI Foundations, lesson 16.
Jordan: Let's start with the bare bones. A linear model calculates a score. It multiplies each input feature by a weight, sums them up, and adds an intercept.
Alex: For linear regression, that score is your final prediction. For logistic regression, you squash that score through a sigmoid function to get a probability between zero and one.
Jordan: And both use the loss minimization and gradient descent we covered back in lesson 11. Now picture what this looks like geometrically for a classifier. You pick a probability threshold, say 0.5. Everywhere the score hits that threshold is your decision boundary. In two dimensions, that boundary is a straight line. In three dimensions, a flat plane. In a thousand dimensions, a hyperplane.
Alex: And moving your classification threshold just slides that hyperplane back and forth parallel to itself. It never tilts or rotates.
Jordan: Which connects straight back to lesson 13. Tuning a threshold trades precision for recall, but if your boundary is angled the wrong way, sliding it back and forth won't fix your errors.
Alex: Because the problem might not be linearly separable.
Jordan: That's the famous XOR limit that Minsky and Papert proved back in 1969. If you have four points where the label is positive only when exactly one of two switches is flipped, no single straight line can split the positives from the negatives.
Alex: Linear models can't handle interactions. They can't capture a rule where the effect of feature A depends entirely on feature B. Or non-monotonic effects, where a little bit of something is good, but a lot is bad.
Jordan: Think of a credit scorecard. That's literally a linear model under the hood. You get plus twelve points for high cash flow, minus eight points for a missed payment. Each line item adds or subtracts independently. The scorecard can't say "occupancy only matters if the lease term is short" unless an analyst writes a dedicated line item for that specific combination.
Alex: But that's the escape hatch, isn't it? Linear models are linear in their parameters, not in the raw data.
Jordan: Exactly. You can engineer features. You can feed in the product of two features, or the log of a feature, or square it. The model stays strictly linear and easy to fit, but its boundary bends around the raw inputs. A deep neural network is just automating that process. It stacks nonlinear layers to construct a rich feature space, and the final layer is just a linear readout.
Alex: We also have to be careful reading the weights. A weight tells you the change in score per unit of that feature, holding everything else constant. But if two features are correlated, they split the credit unpredictably.
Jordan: Right. Drop one correlated feature, and the other's weight might double overnight, even if the model's accuracy doesn't budge.
Alex: We saw this play out in Anthropic's 2024 sleeper agents paper. They deliberately trained models to act safe until a secret trigger made them write insecure code.
Jordan: And then they tested whether a simple linear probe on the residual stream could catch the model preparing to defect.
Alex: And it worked surprisingly well. The network did all the complex nonlinear processing to extract meaning, and the probe just drew a single hyperplane through the activation space. Anthropic even built effective probes just by contrasting activations from yes-no prompts about danger.
Jordan: But keep their caveats in mind. Those models had synthetic backdoors baked into them. Emergent deception in the wild might not be so cleanly separable. Plus, just because a probe can read a direction doesn't prove the model actually uses that direction to make decisions.
Alex: That's the linear representation hypothesis. The idea that models organize concepts as linear directions. It's powerful, but still debated.
Jordan: Time to test this. Quiz time, Alex. Question one: You model corn yield with linear regression using rainfall and June soil temperature. Agronomy says extra rain boosts yield on warm soil, but hurts crops on cold, waterlogged soil. What does the rainfall weight end up looking like, and how do you fix it?
Alex: The rainfall weight will probably hover near zero or look completely misleading, because the positive effect on warm soil cancels out the negative effect on cold soil. To fix it, you could add an interaction term, multiplying rain by temperature, so the linear model gets that combined signal. Or you switch to a nonlinear model like gradient-boosted trees that can split on temperature first.
Jordan: Spot on. Question two: A paper claims a linear probe on layer twenty detects whether a model is about to call an external tool with 97% accuracy. What checks do you want to see before believing the model represents tool intent linearly?
Alex: First, check the class balance and baseline. If 95% of tokens never call a tool, a dummy classifier gets 95% accuracy by doing nothing. Second, run controls, like training the probe on shuffled labels or random weights to make sure the probe isn't doing the heavy lifting itself. Third, test on genuinely held-out tasks and phrasings. And fourth, a causal test: steer the model along that probe's direction and see if tool calling actually changes.
Jordan: Decoding a signal doesn't prove causality. Last one: Your email fraud filter has a large positive weight on the word "wire". A coworker drops the feature "urgent payment", and the weight on "wire" doubles, while overall F1 score stays flat. What happened, and is "wire" alone a solid fraud signal?
Alex: That's collinearity. "Wire" and "urgent payment" were correlated and sharing credit. When one vanished, the other absorbed its share. And no, "wire" alone isn't reliable. It only looked that strong because the urgency phrasing was removed from the model.
