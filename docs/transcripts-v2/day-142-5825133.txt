Jordan: Let's start with the baseline alternative: one-hot encoding. If your vocabulary has fifty thousand words, every word is a vector of forty-nine thousand nine hundred and ninety-nine zeros and a single one.
Alex: Which means rent is just as far from lease as it is from tractor. There is zero shared meaning.
Jordan: None. An embedding replaces that sparse list with a short, dense vector, say 768 decimal numbers. Mechanically, the embedding layer is just a matrix where each row is a word. Multiplying a one-hot vector by that matrix literally just looks up that row. Backpropagation tunes those numbers during training.
Alex: But nobody is manually labeling dimension 42 as legal terms and dimension 105 as farming. Where does the actual geometry come from?
Jordan: Mostly contrastive learning. The model sees pairs that belong together, like a question and its answer, and training pulls their vectors closer while shoving random negatives away. Think of a librarian who never reads the books, but only watches what people borrow together. If patrons consistently check out two books in the same visit, the librarian shelves them side by side.
Alex: That works until patron habits betray you. An email saying payment received and an email saying payment not received appear in the exact same threads, surrounded by the exact same words.
Jordan: Precisely. The librarian shelves them together because their context looks identical. That brings us to how we compare these vectors. We use cosine similarity, which measures the angle between them from minus one to one. If your vectors are normalized to length one, cosine similarity is just a simple dot product.
Alex: And you warned us back in lesson 18: cosine similarity is not a probability. A score of 0.82 does not mean an eighty-two percent chance of a match.
Jordan: Right. Each model has its own score distribution. You cannot borrow thresholds between models. Now, inside an LLM, every individual token gets its own embedding vector that gets updated as layers process context. An embedding model takes those contextualized vectors across the whole document and pools them, often by averaging them, into a single vector for the entire text.
Alex: That final pooled vector is what we save into our vector database. And some models let you truncate that vector, right?
Jordan: Matryoshka representation learning. The model forces the most critical details into the first few dozen dimensions. Models like Voyage 3.5 or OpenAI's text-embedding-3 let you chop a vector down from thousands of dimensions to 256. You lose a tiny bit of retrieval quality, but you save massive amounts of RAM and disk space.
Alex: Walk through what happens when an email arrives in a real pipeline using text-embedding-3-small. Take the line: Q3 cash rent due from north parcel tenant.
Jordan: First, the text gets tokenized. Next, each token ID grabs its initial row from the embedding lookup table. Then the transformer layers process the whole sequence, pulling the word rent toward land leases rather than renting movies. Finally, the model pools and normalizes those activations into a single 1536-dimensional vector. When you calculate the dot product against your database, farmland lease payment schedule scores near the top, while tractor repair scores near zero.
