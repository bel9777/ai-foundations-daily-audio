As we covered in Lesson 22 on training deep networks reliably, building and serving frontier models requires staggering amounts of capital. If investors demand real profits and pull back on compute budgets, that cheap compute dries up. Model providers might double per-token prices, cut smaller, less profitable models, or pull back on free tiers. It is worth asking yourself today: if your API bill doubled tomorrow, does your automation pipeline still make economic sense?

Jordan: It was a rough stretch between November 12 and 17. Microsoft 365, the FAA air-traffic network, AWS us-east-1, Chase, United Airlines, and Apple all dealt with major outages.
Alex: People immediately started whispering about coordinated cyberattacks. Was it hackers?
Jordan: Mostly no. According to The Conversation, which tracked seven major tech incidents across the week, only one was a confirmed cyberattack: a supply-chain compromise of Optimizely, the web experimentation platform. 
Alex: What did the attackers do with it?
Jordan: One security analysis found that on November 16, attackers used the compromised Optimizely script to inject card-skimming code into roughly 178 websites. 
Alex: That is terrifying because companies embed those scripts without thinking twice. 
Jordan: Exactly. That third-party script you never look at becomes the vulnerability. For your own unattended automations, this week is a wake-up call to harden your jobs. Add exponential backoff so your scripts do not slam an unstable API. Add a circuit breaker that alerts you and halts after several consecutive errors instead of looping forever. Make operations idempotent, so if a network drops and a script retries, it does not send duplicate emails or double-charge an account. And pin your dependency versions.
Alex: That connects back to our lab on model confidence. When an automated system fails without monitoring, it fails silently while pretending everything is fine. You need logs and hard stops.
Jordan: Exactly.
