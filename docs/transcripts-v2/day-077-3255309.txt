Jordan: Spot on. Think of prompt injection as a Trojan horse. Your AI is reading an email or a document, and hidden in that text is a command that says, ignore your instructions and do this instead. If your AI is connected to tools—like the ability to send emails or change passwords—that hidden command becomes a real-world problem.
Alex: Okay, so if the AI thinks the untrusted text is a command, it might go off the rails. How do we keep that from becoming a catastrophe?
Jordan: That is where the concept of least privilege comes in. Imagine your AI is a guest in your house. You don’t give them the key to your safe, the garage code, and your bank login just because they’re there to help you summarize a book. You give them access to the bookshelf and that’s it.
Alex: So if the AI gets hacked or just makes a massive logic error, it physically can’t do damage because it doesn’t have the permissions to touch the sensitive stuff.
Jordan: Exactly. And even with those limits, you should always have human approval and guardrails. If the system is doing something high-stakes, like sending money or firing off a legal document, make it stop and ask a human to click a button.
Alex: It’s like a circuit breaker. If the AI goes too far, the human is the one who flips the switch back. But how do we know if it’s working?
Jordan: You have to log and monitor everything. Logging is just keeping a record of what the system saw and what it decided to do. Monitoring is watching those logs so you can spot spikes in weird behavior. Without logs, you’re just guessing why your system crashed.
Alex: Let’s put this into a real-world scenario. Say, a clinic’s AI assistant that summarizes patient messages. How do these pieces fit together there?
Jordan: First, the threat model identifies that someone might try to trick the AI into leaking health data. Finally, we log every single draft so we can audit the system if something goes wrong.
Alex: It’s not one big silver bullet, it’s just layering these habits.

Alex: Safety is about the accidents—the model making a mistake because of bad design or simple bugs. Security is about the bad actors—people actively trying to break in or manipulate the system.
