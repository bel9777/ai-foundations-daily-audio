Jordan: Today is about features, which is the bridge between the data audit you ran last week and the metrics you want to hit. A feature is just a measurable property of an example, available right at the moment of prediction, converted into a number.
Alex: Because the real world doesn't come neatly packaged in floating-point vectors.
Jordan: It really doesn't. A feature is an editorial decision you make. Take raw data types. For a numeric value like an invoice amount, you might take the log so a rogue million-dollar bill does not blow up the weights. If you have a categorical value like vendor names, you cannot just label them one, two, and three. That invents an artificial order where vendor three is somehow greater than vendor one. You use one-hot encoding instead.
Alex: And dates are tricky too. A raw timestamp just makes the model think later dates mean bigger numbers.
Jordan: Right. You transform a date into something actionable, like days until payment is due, or you turn the day of the week into sine and cosine coordinates so Sunday sits right next to Monday. That is encoding. Feature engineering goes a step further by injecting domain knowledge. A raw invoice for fourteen thousand dollars tells the model very little. But if you engineer a feature that says this is 3.8 times the vendor's historical median, suddenly the model has context.
Alex: Even sophisticated models cannot figure that out if each row arrives in isolation without that vendor history attached.
Jordan: Exactly. Think of real estate appraisers. Nobody judges a commercial building raw. They calculate capitalization rate: net operating income divided by property price. That single engineered ratio normalizes buildings of totally different sizes. 
Alex: What about deep learning? People say modern neural networks learn their own features.
Jordan: They do. An embedding is literally a learned feature vector borrowed from another model. Mechanistic interpretability research tries to reverse-engineer those internal representations. Anthropic did that in 2023 with sparse autoencoders, pulling out individual monosemantic concepts from inside models, and researchers pushed that further in mid-2026 with cosine-scored SAEs. But whether we engineer the numbers by hand or borrow learned vectors, the rules of reality still apply: availability and train-serve parity.
Alex: Which means you cannot use features from the future, and you have to generate them in production the exact same way you did in training.
Jordan: Precisely. If you use the seller's final accepted price to predict what a building will sell for, that is data leakage. If you train on trailing net income but your live loop uses projected pro-forma income, that is train-serve skew. It looks great in offline evaluation and crashes in production.
Alex: People might look at new tabular foundation models like Prior Labs' TabPFN or Google's TabFM and think tabular features do not matter anymore since those models work on spreadsheets zero-shot.
Jordan: Those models replace manual algorithm tuning, not human domain knowledge. If you feed TabPFN an invoice table, you still have to drop the approved-by column because that only gets populated after human review. If you leave it in, it leaks the label. You still need to calculate vendor prior counts and check if the amount is a round number. The model only sees the columns you give it.

Jordan: First scenario. You are predicting which university lease renewals will require a rent concession. Your raw fields are current rent, market rent per square foot, tenant ID, concession amount, months remaining, and lease start date. What do you drop, what do you transform, and what is the first engineered feature you build?
Alex: Drop concession amount immediately. That is the outcome we are trying to predict, so having it in the input is pure leakage. Drop tenant ID too, or at least raw ID, because one-hot encoding hundreds of unique tenants just encourages the model to memorize individuals. For transforms, convert lease start date into tenant tenure in years, and keep months remaining as a clean numeric interval. For the first engineered feature, I would build an above-market ratio: current rent divided by the product of market rent per square foot and total square footage. That directly captures pricing pressure, just like cap rate.
Jordan: Spot on. Scenario two: your email triage classifier was trained on OpenAI text-embedding-3-small vectors. To protect user privacy, you swap the live pipeline to run EmbeddingGemma 2 locally, but you leave your trained logistic regression classifier untouched. What happens?
Alex: Total silent failure. The dimensions might not even match, and even if they do, dimension 412 in OpenAI does not mean the same thing as dimension 412 in Gemma. The model will output confident garbage. To migrate, you have to re-embed your entire historical training set with Gemma, retrain the classifier, verify your precision and recall on a holdout split, and tag your stored vectors with the model version.
Jordan: Exactly right. That is textbook train-serve skew. Third scenario: a feed-cost forecaster on a farm worked great in backtests using a seven-day average consumption feature, but in production it keeps drifting off. You check the code and find the notebook used rolling seven, center equals true. What went wrong?
Alex: Centered rolling averages peek three days into the future. At training time, the model knew Wednesday's and Thursday's numbers while predicting Tuesday. In production, you only have past data. So training had future leakage, and production has train-serve skew because the live window is trailing, not centered. The fix is strictly sorting by date and using a trailing seven-day rolling window, preferably imported from the exact same shared python script in both training and production.
