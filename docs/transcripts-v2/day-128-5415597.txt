Alex: Did you see that preprint on arXiv last week? Big Claims, Small Margins.
Jordan: ArXiv 2610.28714. It took dead aim at a whole corner of the field, specifically graph neural networks that use LLMs to classify text.
Alex: Where every new paper claimed state-of-the-art accuracy.
Jordan: Exactly. The researchers reran simple, cheap baselines on the exact same data splits. Once the baselines were tested fairly, those massive reported gains basically shrank to nothing. It mirrors another paper from February showing simple baselines are competitive for model diffing.
Alex: If teams of researchers can fool themselves with weak baselines, anyone building a solo automation will do it twice as fast.
Jordan: Much faster, because nobody is peer-reviewing your prompt.
Alex: If a five-line keyword rule catches ninety percent of what your expensive model catches, you are paying frontier prices for the last ten percent. This is AI Foundations, lesson 15.
Jordan: So what is a baseline? It is the simplest reasonable method evaluated on the exact same data, split, and metric as your candidate.
Alex: Break down the common flavors.
Jordan: Four main types. First, trivial baselines. Always predict the majority class, the mean, or whatever the value was yesterday. Zero features used. Second, heuristic baselines. That is a ten-minute rule from a domain expert, like if the sender is on your VIP list, flag it as urgent.
Alex: What about whatever code is already running in production?
Jordan: That is the incumbent baseline. Last month's prompt, an old regex, or a smaller model. The fourth is a human baseline, which usually acts as an upper benchmark rather than a floor.
Alex: Speaking of floors, how do you actually measure progress against them?
Jordan: Think in terms of floor, ceiling, and headroom. The baseline is your floor. The human expert or theoretical maximum is your ceiling. Headroom is the gap between them. You measure progress by the share of headroom your model closed: your model's score minus the baseline, divided by the ceiling minus the baseline.
Alex: Right. If the floor is eighty-eight percent and the ceiling is ninety-six, jumping to ninety-two sounds decent on paper. But mathematically, you only closed half the open headroom.
Jordan: Exactly. And some metrics bake the baseline straight into the formula. Those are skill scores. You already use R-squared in regression, which compares your error to a dummy model predicting the mean. In forecasting, people use MASE, Mean Absolute Scaled Error. It divides your forecast error by the error of a naive guess. If your MASE is over one, looking up yesterday's number literally beat your model.
Alex: That is embarrassing. But to make any of this honest, you need what you call baseline hygiene.
Jordan: Four non-negotiable rules. Both methods use the exact same test examples. Both use the exact same metric. Both get comparable tuning effort. And neither is chosen by peeking at the test set. The most common trap is tuning asymmetry. People test twenty prompt variations against one sloppy regex they wrote while half-asleep.
Alex: And they declare the LLM the winner. You also have to track cost and latency, because a baseline that is two points lower but five hundred times cheaper is usually the real winner.
Jordan: Always. That is why you climb the baseline ladder in order: constant, rule, classical model like logistic regression, small LLM, then frontier LLM. Stop climbing the moment the next rung costs more than the value it adds. It is like Wall Street. Nobody praises a fund manager for returning nine percent if the S and P five hundred did eleven. Alpha only exists relative to the index.
Alex: Look at ARC-AGI-3 for a wild real-world example of this.
Jordan: ARC-AGI-3 tests AI agents on game environments against human efficiency, calibrated against hundreds of people. A competitor named Psyho pointed out that the organizers changed the human reference from the second-best player to the median player, and bumped the cap to one-fifteen percent. The agents did nothing different, but their scores jumped. One hundred percent suddenly meant as good as an average person instead of a near-expert.
Alex: Shift the baseline, shift the narrative. You see the same thing with foundation models for time series, like Chronos. Benchmarks like Air Quality Arena compare them against Seasonal Naive, which just predicts whatever happened the same season last year. If you cannot beat that, you do not have a product.
Jordan: Time to see if you can spot bad baselines in the wild. Quiz time.
Alex: Bring it on.
Jordan: Scenario one. You build a model to forecast monthly utility bills across forty campus buildings. Your MAE is four hundred ten dollars. Last-month persistence is four hundred fifty-five. But same-month-last-year is three hundred eighty. Your coworker says the model beats the baseline. What do you say?
Alex: I tell them they picked the wrong baseline to make the model look good. Campus utilities are totally seasonal. Winter heating is not like autumn weather. Against seasonal naive, our MASE is over one, roughly one point zero eight. The simple lookup beat us. Next, I would give the model heating and cooling degree days, or just predict the difference from last year's bill.
Jordan: Clean diagnosis. Scenario two. Your guest-intake classifier is ninety-one percent accurate. But eighty-seven percent of incoming mail is no-action spam. The LLM costs fourteen bucks a month, and a basic sender rule costs zero. How do you set up the evaluation?
Alex: Accuracy is lying because of class imbalance. I would evaluate the majority guess, the sender rule, and the LLM on macro-F1 and recall on the action class. Then I calculate the headroom closed and see what each missed action email actually costs us. If the extra catches are not worth fourteen dollars plus the code maintenance, the rule wins. Or we do a hybrid: rule handles obvious spam, LLM takes the edge cases.
Jordan: Nailed it. Last one. You tested fifteen prompts on a set of one hundred twenty invoices, picked the best prompt, and claimed it beat a regex baseline by six points. What are the two fatal flaws?
Alex: First, test-set leakage. You picked the winning prompt on the same data you used to evaluate it, so you overfit to those specific invoices. Second, tuning asymmetry. You tuned the prompt fifteen times and wrote the regex once. To fix it, split the data, tune both with equal effort on a dev split, test once on a held-out set, and put error bars on it. Six points on one hundred twenty items is only seven invoices. That could be pure luck.
Jordan: Spot on across the board. Here is your homework. Pull one or two hundred past items your automation already labeled, like Gmail triage or invoice categories. Verify the true labels. Write a ten-minute heuristic rule and do not touch it after seeing results. Run majority-class, your rule, and your LLM on accuracy, macro-F1, and headroom closed. Notice how close majority accuracy is to the LLM, and calculate whether the LLM actually earns its monthly invoice on rare-class recall. Then write a one-line summary in your project README stating what the LLM beats the rule by, and at what dollar cost.
