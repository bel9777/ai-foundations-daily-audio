Alex: What caught my eye was how it framed representation. We tend to imagine there is a single neuron dedicated to a specific idea, like a loan default neuron, but concepts are actually smeared across patterns of many neurons. A network can represent far more features than its physical neuron count.

Jordan: Let us start with that single unit: the artificial neuron. It takes in inputs, multiplies each by a weight, adds them together with a bias, and passes that sum through an activation function. If that activation is the sigmoid function, you have built logistic regression. That is the entire foundation.
Alex: So a deep neural network is just logistic regression units wired into each other?
Jordan: Pretty much. The outputs of one set of neurons feed into the next set. We call any layer between your raw inputs and your final prediction a hidden layer. But here is the catch: you must have a nonlinear activation function like tanh, ReLU, or GELU between them.
Alex: Because if you just chain linear equations, matrix algebra collapses them into one single linear equation.
Jordan: Right. W two times W one times x is just some combined matrix W three times x. Without nonlinearity, stacking ten layers gives you nothing more than a standard linear model. With nonlinearity, Cybenko and Hornik proved back around 1990 that even a single hidden layer can approximate any reasonable continuous function, given enough neurons. Mind you, that theorem does not guarantee you can train it easily, or that it will generalize to new data.
Alex: How do we actually train it?
Jordan: We measure our error using a loss function, typically log loss for classification. Then backpropagation, popularized by Rumelhart, Hinton, and Williams in 1986, uses the calculus chain rule to work backward from that loss, figuring out how much each individual weight contributed to the error. Finally, gradient descent tweaks every parameter by a small step to reduce that error.
Alex: You had a great credit committee analogy for this.
Jordan: Picture a loan committee. Each hidden neuron is an analyst scoring an applicant using a personal checklist. They only speak up if the score crosses a threshold; that is your activation function. The output neuron is the committee chair, who weighs each analyst's opinion to make the final call. When a loan goes bad, backpropagation is the post-mortem. The chair sends blame back to each analyst based on how much their input swayed the decision, and everyone adjusts their checklist.
Alex: And unlike logistic regression, the loss landscape here is non-convex.
Jordan: Exactly. In logistic regression, there is one smooth bowl, so you always slide down to the single best minimum. In a neural network, depending on where your random starting weights land, gradient descent can get trapped in a mediocre local valley.
Alex: Before we jump into the numbers, when someone says a model has millions of parameters, that is just every single weight and bias counted up?
Jordan: That is it. Take the smallest public release of OpenAI's GPT-2 from 2019. It has twelve layers, and each layer contains an attention block and a multilayer perceptron block. That MLP block takes a token vector of 768 numbers, passes it through a hidden layer of 3,072 neurons with GELU activations, and projects it back out to 768 numbers.
Alex: Let me do the math on that. 768 inputs times 3,072 hidden weights, plus 3,072 biases, plus 3,072 times 768 output weights, plus 768 output biases. That is about 4.7 million parameters in a single block.
Jordan: Multiply that by twelve layers, and you get around 56.7 million parameters. That means roughly 45 percent of GPT-2 small is just these basic feed-forward neurons. Modern models scale this up with tricks like mixture of experts, but the core mechanism is identical.
