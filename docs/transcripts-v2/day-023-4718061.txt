Alex: If computers can only crunch numbers, the only way they can ever understand human concepts is if we turn meaning itself into a map. This is AI Foundations, day 23. Today we are talking about embeddings, which is one of those buzzwords people throw around without actually explaining what is going on.
Jordan: It is, but the core idea is simple. An embedding is a learned numeric representation of an item, like a word, product, user, or image patch. Similar things end up with similar numbers. In machine learning, we call that a geometry of meaning.
Alex: Back up for a second. Why do we need this whole geometric setup? If you have a basic database, cat is one, dog is two, banana is three. Why are raw numeric identifiers not enough for a neural network?
Jordan: Think about the assumptions in those numbers. If cat is one, dog is two, and banana is three, the math says dog is halfway between cat and banana. It implies a banana is three times a cat. Those fake distances and false orders do not exist in reality.
Alex: Right, which links straight back to day 20 when we talked about representations. If the way you encode your input data does not match the actual structure of the problem, the model struggles to learn anything useful.
Jordan: Exactly. A single label squashes everything onto a line where things are forced together arbitrarily. To fix that, we represent each item with a vector. A vector is simply an ordered list of numbers, like zero point two, minus one point four, three point zero.
Alex: And geometrically, a vector is just coordinates pointing to a specific location in space.
Jordan: That is it. Once items are points on a map, the model can treat items near each other as similar. Usually, we measure closeness using Euclidean distance, which is just standard straight-line distance. In an embedding space, cat and dog land close together. King and queen land close together. Banana and airplane end up on opposite sides of the room.
Alex: But nobody is sitting at a keyboard hand-crafting coordinates for fifty thousand words or millions of products. How does the model actually figure out where to place them?
Jordan: It learns them during training, starting with random vectors. When the model makes a prediction, it gets an error signal from the loss function. Then backpropagation, from day 21, assigns blame and tells the network how to nudge each coordinate in the vector to reduce that error.
Alex: And connecting to day 22, it repeats that process across thousands of batches using small, stable updates. Over time, items that appear in similar contexts or serve similar purposes get nudged into the same neighborhood.
Jordan: Exactly. Picture a giant library map. If millions of books have random shelf numbers, the numbers tell you nothing. But if history books sit in one wing, novels in another, and physics in another, you can navigate by topic. The coordinates are not definitions, but they organize meaning in a way the model can navigate.
Alex: That library image really helps. How does this look in practice outside of language, say, on a shopping site?
Jordan: Online stores use them constantly. Product vectors place running shoes near athletic socks and water bottles because shoppers buy them together. Customer vectors capture habits like budget and style. To recommend items, the system just looks for product vectors sitting close to your customer vector.
Alex: And the system does not need a human-style understanding of what running shoes actually are. It just relies on those geometric patterns to make predictions.
Jordan: Precisely. Beyond recommendations, embeddings show up in three major places. First, categories like city names, product IDs, or user IDs. Second, search and retrieval, where a search query and a relevant document get placed near each other. And third, words and tokens in language models.
Alex: Remind us what a token is again.
Jordan: A token is a chunk of text that a model processes. It could be a full word, part of a word, or punctuation. Giving each token an embedding vector solves the sparsity problem. In older setups, one-hot encoding created massive vectors that were mostly empty zeros. Embeddings are compact and dense, packing rich relationships into a manageable space.
Alex: So the big idea to remember is that embeddings turn abstract items into points in space where distance and direction capture meaningful relationships.
