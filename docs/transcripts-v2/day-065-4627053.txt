Alex: Most AI projects don't fail because the model is broken; they fail because the team simply runs out of useful data. This is AI Foundations, day 65.
Jordan: And you do not have to sit around waiting for some mythical, perfect dataset to fall from the sky. That is where synthetic data comes in.
Alex: Synthetic data sounds fancy, but in plain terms, it just means fake data, right? Information we manufactured instead of scraping from the messy real world.
Jordan: Pretty much, yeah. It is data generated by code, rules, simulations, or another AI model. If you are building an automated invoice reader, you do not need to hunt down ten thousand actual customer invoices with real credit cards on them. You can write a script that spits out ten thousand fake ones.
Alex: Which solves a massive privacy headache right out of the gate. But how do teams actually create this stuff without making a mess?
Jordan: There are three main flavors. First, augmented examples. You take a real sentence or record and tweak it slightly—change names, swap dates, rephrase words, but keep the label intact.
Alex: And the label is just the ground truth answer attached to an example, like marking an email as spam or categorizing a support ticket.
Jordan: Exactly. The second flavor is simulated examples, generated from an environment or process. Think of a 3D driving simulator where self-driving software navigates rainstorms or crazy lane merges. The third is model-generated examples, where you literally prompt a capable language model to dream up edge cases, summaries, or customer queries.
Alex: That covers speed, privacy, and cost. Labeling real data is brutally expensive, and waiting for real events takes forever. But you also mentioned coverage.
Jordan: Coverage is probably the biggest win. A rare case might only happen once every few months in your real logs, like a bizarre billing dispute. But that rare case matters a lot when it hits. With synthetic data, you can generate five hundred variations of that exact rare case before lunch.
Alex: If it is so cheap and fast, why even bother collecting real data? Why not build the entire system in a digital sandbox?
Jordan: Because a sandbox is not the real world. Think of synthetic data like an empty football practice field. You have painted lines, plastic cones, dummy tacklers, and rehearsed plays. It is brilliant for footwork, timing, and preparing for unusual plays. But it is not game day. Cones do not hit back. Real opponents, bad weather, crowd noise, and real-time panic behave completely differently.
Alex: If you only ever hit plastic dummies, you get crushed the second a real linebacker runs at you.
Jordan: You fold instantly. In AI, that risk is known as model collapse or data drift toward the model's own habits. If you take a flawed model, generate mountains of synthetic data with it, and train a new model on that synthetic data, the new model copies and magnifies every single blind spot. It starts learning from its own reflections instead of reality. That is why synthetic data is a helper, not a substitute. You must mix it with real data and keep real data for your benchmarks.
Alex: Which brings us to the data flywheel. How does that keep the system grounded?
Jordan: The flywheel is the ongoing loop that uses real life to make the model smarter over time. People use your AI product. That usage generates signals. You turn those signals into training or evaluation data. You retrain or tune prompts, the product gets noticeably better, and that attracts more usage. The cycle keeps spinning.
Alex: Break down those signals. What are we actually watching for while people use the tool?
Jordan: You have implicit signals and explicit signals. Implicit signals come from natural user behavior. Did they copy the AI's answer, or did they immediately delete it and rephrase their question? Did they abandon the task? Those are easy to harvest at scale, but they can be ambiguous.
Alex: Because you do not always know why they abandoned it. Maybe the doorbell rang.
Jordan: Right. Explicit signals, on the other hand, are deliberate feedback. Thumbs up, star ratings, or direct edits to the text. They are clearer about intent, but users leave them far less often. A great flywheel tracks both: implicit signals tell you what users do, explicit signals tell you why.
Alex: Put this together for me with the customer support assistant from the lesson. How do synthetic data and the flywheel work side by side?
Jordan: Say you start with a few thousand real past chat logs. That covers common issues. Then you use synthetic data to manufacture hundreds of rare, messy edge cases—like tricky refund disputes or shipping mix-ups. You deploy the assistant to your support staff. When an agent clicks send on a suggested reply, that is an implicit thumbs up. When an agent rewrites the reply, that edit becomes an explicit, high-value training example for the next model update.
Alex: And that ties right back to Day 64 on retrieval. We can generate synthetic questions to test whether our retrieval system pulls the right policy document before any real customer sees it.
Jordan: Exactly. You can even use synthetic data to test conversational memory by simulating long, messy chats full of interruptions. Just remember Day 60: tie it to actual business metrics. The goal is never just generating more data. The goal is cutting handle time, reducing errors, or saving manual effort.
Alex: Keep your eyes on the metric, not the volume. Alright, quiz me before I get too comfortable.
Jordan: Question one: what is synthetic data in plain language?
Alex: It is data that is generated artificially—using code, simulations, rules, or other AI models—instead of being collected directly from real-world events.
Jordan: Question two: why can synthetic data be dangerous if you rely on it carelessly?
Alex: Because it can act like an echo chamber. If the generator has flaws, synthetic data copies and magnifies those mistakes, leading to model collapse where the system learns its own habits instead of actual reality.
Jordan: Right on target. Question three: what is a data flywheel, and what role do user signals play in it?
Alex: It is a self-reinforcing loop where product usage produces user signals, those signals become data to retrain and improve the model, and the better model brings more usage. Implicit actions and explicit feedback are the fuel telling you what to fix.
