Alex: That makes sense. But what happens when a model learns the wrong pattern entirely? Like, it gets the right answer on the practice set, but for a totally absurd reason?
Jordan: That is called overfitting, and it is one of the most common traps in machine learning. Imagine you want a model to identify cows in photos. In your dataset, every picture of a cow happens to be standing in a lush green pasture. The non-cow pictures are all indoors or on sandy roads. The model looks for the easiest shortcut, so it learns that green grass equals cow.
Alex: So if I show it a picture of a cow inside a barn, it says no cow.
Jordan: Right. And if you show it an empty golf course, it yells cow. It did not learn what a cow looks like. It overfit to the accidental noise in your dataset. It is like a student practicing driving on one quiet suburban street. They look great until you put them on a highway in heavy rain.
Alex: Okay, so how do we catch a model before it starts mistaking golf courses for cows?
Jordan: We split our data into separate groups. The standard setup is three buckets: a training set, a validation set, and a test set.
Alex: Like a 70-15-15 split?
Jordan: Yeah, that is a common ratio. The model studies the seventy percent training set to learn patterns. While we build and tweak the model, we check it against the fifteen percent validation set to see which settings work best.
Alex: And the test set is locked in a vault until the very end.
Jordan: Exactly. The test set is the final exam. You cannot peek at it while training, and you cannot change your model based on its test score. If you keep tweaking your answers to fit the final exam, it is not a test anymore. You just leaked the answers into your study time.

Jordan: Perfect. And if you are building a chatbot or generative AI, metrics get even trickier. You end up measuring factual accuracy, tone, safety, or whether it actually followed instructions. You always have to answer the question: good at what?
