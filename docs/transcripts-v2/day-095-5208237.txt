Alex: Building an AI that works in a notebook is one thing, but if it drains your bank account or takes twenty seconds to answer, it is dead on arrival. This is AI Foundations, day 95.
Jordan: That is the whole ballgame today. Up until now, we have mostly focused on whether a model can do the job. Now we have to ask if the system can do that job repeatedly, safely, and affordably in the real world.
Alex: Which is where deployment comes in. We hear that word constantly in software, but what does it actually mean for AI?
Jordan: In a notebook or lab, a model is just an experiment. Deployment means turning that model into a live service that real users or automated workflows rely on every day. Once you do that, you run headfirst into three big operational constraints: latency, throughput, and reliability.
Alex: Latency is how fast the user gets an answer, throughput is how many total requests the system handles at once, and reliability is whether it actually stays up and gives consistent answers without crashing.
Jordan: Right. And they constantly fight each other. If you build a complex pipeline that double-checks every fact, your answers might be sharper, but your latency shoots up and your throughput drops. You serve fewer people and they wait longer.
Alex: And every single one of those interactions costs real money.
Jordan: That brings us to the atomic unit of deployment: the request. One query, one API call, one specific task. Teams often forget that a request is almost never just sending text to a model and getting text back.
Alex: Walk me through what else is happening behind the curtain.
Jordan: In a real application, a single user request kicks off an entire pipeline. First, you validate the input to make sure it is safe. Then you might run a retrieval step to pull relevant facts from a database. Maybe the model calls a tool to look up account details. Then the model generates an answer. Then you run safety checks on that output, format it, and log the whole exchange for auditing.
Alex: So the compute cost of the model itself is just one slice of the bill. You are paying for database queries, external API calls, storage, network traffic, and maybe even human review when the system gets confused.
Jordan: Exactly. That is why deployment is fundamentally an economic problem, not just a technical one. You have to balance the cost per request against the value per request, especially when you scale from ten users to ten thousand.
Alex: You can build the most brilliant AI agent in tech history, but if each query costs fifty cents to run and generates two cents of business value, you built an expensive party trick.
Jordan: Think of it like opening a restaurant. Having an incredible recipe is not enough. You need a kitchen that can prep dishes fast enough when the dinner rush hits. You need staff to handle the floor, quality control so the soup tastes the same every Tuesday, and menu prices that keep the lights on. A brilliant chef who takes two hours to make a single plate goes out of business.
Alex: That puts the engineering choices in perspective. It means we have to choose where to compromise.
Jordan: We call those the three knobs: quality, speed, and cost. You almost never get to turn all three up to maximum. If you want maximum quality, you might run larger models, multiple verification passes, and extra retrieval steps. But your speed drops and your cost spikes. If you cut corners to make it dirt cheap and lightning fast, your quality and safety fall apart.
Alex: It is an optimization puzzle. You dial in the exact balance your specific product needs. And that balance does not freeze once you hit launch day, right?
Jordan: Not at all. Evaluation has to run continuously. Once real users touch the system, you need active monitoring to track how it behaves under real traffic. That is how you catch things like drift.
Alex: Drift is when the real world changes, but the system stays stuck in the past.
Jordan: Exactly. Say you run a customer support assistant. When you built it, you tested it against your company policy. Six months later, the company changes its return policy, or users start asking about a brand new product that was not around during testing. The model starts failing or hallucinating because the input distribution shifted. If you are not monitoring for drift, you will not notice until customers start complaining.
Alex: Let us tie this together with that customer support assistant. Over the last few lessons, we looked at retrieval, tool use, evaluation, and safety. How do all those pieces collide in a live deployment?
Jordan: Picture a support bot handling shipping, refunds, and account lockouts. It uses retrieval to grab current policy text. It uses tool use to check order tracking in an internal database. It uses safety guards to block prompt injections and avoid leaking someone else's personal data. And it relies on evaluation benchmarks to verify that its answers are grounded in real policy.
Alex: But the economics dictate how fancy you can get with any of those parts.
Jordan: Precisely. If that company spends three dollars in model calls to resolve a query about a five-dollar pair of socks, the project is a failure. The winning architecture is rarely the flashiest one with the biggest model. It is the one that is reliable enough, fast enough, and cheap enough to maintain long term.
