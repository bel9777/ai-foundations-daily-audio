Alex: Today we are building a calibration script to test whether our model's confidence numbers are actually telling the truth, so we stop trusting blind guesses in our automated pipelines. This is AI Foundations.
Jordan: Most people have an unattended script somewhere with a line that looks like: if confidence is greater than or equal to 0.9, act. Maybe it sends an email, reconciles a transaction, or pushes data into an Excel sheet. But if the model says it is 90% sure, is it actually right nine times out of ten?
Alex: Usually not. We have seen research going back to 2017 showing modern neural nets are notoriously overconfident. And with language models, verbal confidence can be wild.
Jordan: Exactly. That is why today we are building two lightweight Python tools using only the standard library. First, calib.py, which evaluates accuracy, average confidence, Brier scores, and Expected Calibration Error. Second, collect.py, which runs 40 hand-labeled examples through an API to pull both verbal confidence and raw token log probabilities.
Alex: Let us start with calib.py. What does the script actually do under the hood?
Jordan: It loads your predictions, groups them into confidence bins, and compares how sure the model claimed to be against how often it was right. It also calculates a Brier score, which is just the mean squared error between the stated probability and the actual outcome. Zero is a perfect score. Then it calculates a baseline Brier score, which is what you would get if you just lazily predicted your historical accuracy every single time.
Alex: If the model cannot beat the lazy baseline score, its confidence numbers are essentially useless noise.
Jordan: Spot on. The script also includes a demo flag so you can test it before touching an API. When you run python3 calib.py --demo, it generates 200 synthetic items from a deliberately overconfident model.
Alex: And what should we expect to see in that demo output?
Jordan: You should see an accuracy of 0.675 and a mean confidence of 0.805. That is a positive gap of 13%, meaning it is overconfident. The Brier score is 0.227, which is actually worse than the base-rate score of 0.219. But here is the kicker: in the threshold table at the bottom, as the cutoff threshold goes up to 0.95, accuracy climbs to 0.804.
Alex: That feels like a paradox. How can the score be worse than a constant guess, but filtering by higher confidence still gives higher accuracy?
Jordan: Hold that thought, because I am going to quiz you on that in a minute. Once the demo script runs clean, step two is gathering your own ground truth. Pick one specific binary decision from a pipeline you already run. Maybe it is: does this inbound email require a personal reply within 48 hours? You make an items.csv file with forty rows. Label them YES or NO yourself before running anything.
Alex: And make sure at least 15 of those 40 are the messy, ambiguous edge cases. If you only feed it easy layups, every model looks well calibrated.
Jordan: Exactly. Step three is running collect.py. It sends each row to the OpenAI completions endpoint with temperature set to zero and logprobs enabled. We ask the model to reply on one line with YES or NO followed by a confidence number from zero to 100. The script parses out that verbal number, and it also takes the log probability of the first token, exponentiates it, and grabs the model's underlying token probability.
Alex: So we are collecting two different flavors of confidence side by side: what the model explicitly writes, and the math underneath the token. What should the terminal show while that runs?
Jordan: You will see 40 lines stream past showing the item ID, the model's text choice, the verbal confidence, the token probability, and a one or zero for correctness. When it finishes, you run calib.py on your results twice. Once against the verbal column, once against the token probability column.
Alex: What usually goes wrong here? Where do people trip up?
Jordan: Three main traps. First, your 40 items are way too easy. Your accuracy comes back at 100%, calibration looks flawless, and you learn nothing. Mine your real inbox or error logs for the stuff that actually fails. Second, missing logprobs. If the token probability column comes up blank, your selected model or endpoint might not support logprobs. Anthropic historically did not return them, and some OpenAI setups drop them, so double-check your model parameters. Third, fragile regex parsing. If the model outputs a preamble like "Sure, here is your answer: YES 90", the regex fails and leaves the row empty. Or if it emits a stray quotation mark first, your token probability measures the quote, not the answer.
Alex: Always inspect the raw output column in results.csv to make sure your parser is catching the actual words.
Jordan: Now, let us see if the core concepts stuck. Time for your check questions.
Alex: Fire away.
Jordan: Question one. In our demo, the Brier score was worse than the base-rate guess, yet accuracy still went up when we raised the confidence threshold. How can both be true, and what does that mean for your automation?
Alex: Calibration and ranking are two totally different properties. Calibration asks if the actual numbers mean what they say. The demo numbers were inflated, so as absolute probabilities, they were worse than guessing the average. But ranking asks whether higher scores correspond to harder items. Since the model still ranked easy items higher than hard ones, raising the threshold filtered out errors. For an email loop, it means I can use the score to gate actions, but I cannot treat a 0.9 score as a literal 90% guarantee. I have to look at the measured threshold table.
Jordan: Spot on. Question two. Your verbalized confidence shows an Expected Calibration Error of just 0.04. That looks great. But 38 out of your 40 test items landed in the 0.8 to 1.0 bin. Why should you be cautious about calling this model calibrated?
Alex: Because you only measured one tiny bucket. A low calibration error just means the model's average confidence matched its average accuracy in that single bin. You have zero data on whether its 60s mean 60% or its 70s mean 70%. Plus, with only 40 items, a single mistake swings the percentages wildly. You need more hard items spread across the lower bins before you trust that number.
Jordan: Perfect. Question three. You decide to tweak your prompt or switch to a slightly newer model next week. Does your verified threshold still hold?
Alex: Not a chance. Calibration is not a general property of intelligence; it belongs to that specific model, that exact prompt, and that specific dataset. If you touch the prompt or swap the model, you have to run collect.py again.
Jordan: That is the whole lesson. You know this lab worked when you have an empirical threshold written directly into your project documentation, like auto-act at 0.85, route everything below to a review queue, and re-measure once a month. And if neither confidence metric beats the base rate, you drop the confidence threshold entirely and route on label alone.
