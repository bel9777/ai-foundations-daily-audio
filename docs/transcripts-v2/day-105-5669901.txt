Alex: Whether you are deploying Gemini or filtering your company inbox, you have to build an honest feedback loop or your system will fail in production. This is AI Foundations, lesson 3.
Jordan: Most people think machine learning means feeding raw data into an algorithm and watching intelligence pop out. But the model is only one piece. The actual product is the workflow.
Alex: Walk me through the steps of that loop.
Jordan: First, define the task. What should the system predict, sort, or decide? Second, collect historical examples. Third, create labels, which are the correct answers you want it to imitate. Fourth, split your data so you keep some examples hidden. Fifth, train, tune, or prompt the system. Sixth, evaluate it on that held-out data. And seventh, deploy and monitor it in the real world, catching failures so you can restart the loop.
Alex: So if I build a prompt to triage incoming emails, I am running that exact loop even if I never train a model from scratch.
Jordan: Exactly. Let us ground the vocabulary. Your dataset is your collection of examples—say, forty customer emails or property lease rows. The label is the target outcome, like urgent or spam. The features are the inputs the model can inspect to make its decision, like the sender domain, subject line, or dollar amount.
Alex: And the model is the learned mapping between those features and labels.
Jordan: Right. In a tiny scikit-learn script, that might be a decision tree. In a frontier setup, it is a massive transformer. But either way, you need a metric to decide if it is good enough.
Alex: And accuracy is usually a trap here.
Jordan: A massive trap. If only two percent of your inbound messages are urgent, a dumb model that labels everything as not urgent is ninety-eight percent accurate. And completely useless. You need metrics that track false positives, false negatives, precision, recall, and the cost of being wrong.
Alex: That is why you compare this to training a working dog rather than writing a spreadsheet formula.
Jordan: Exactly. A spreadsheet formula is a tax rule. It executes the exact same logic forever. A learned system is like a farm dog. You show it examples, correct its errors, test it on a new pasture, and keep watching it once it starts working. It behaves according to the examples and incentives it absorbed.
Alex: Let us talk about the split, because this is where people cut corners. Train, validation, and test.
Jordan: Think of training as studying the textbook. Validation is taking practice quizzes to adjust your study habits. The test set is the final exam. Google's developer guides warn that validation and test sets can wear out if you repeatedly tune against them. If you keep peeking at the final exam to tweak your prompt or parameters, you have not built a smarter system. You just memorized the test.
Alex: And order matters. If data arrives over time, a random shuffle is dangerous.
Jordan: Very dangerous. If you randomly shuffle real estate transactions or farm operations across years, next year's market conditions leak backward into your training set. If your work happens in time order, always put your newest examples into the test set.
Alex: What about training versus tuning versus prompting? We are not all training foundation models.
Jordan: There are three tiers. Training changes model weights from scratch. Fine-tuning takes an existing model and updates weights with targeted demonstrations. But for automations, the most practical approach is eval-driven prompting. You keep model weights completely frozen. You just refine instructions, tools, and examples against a held-out evaluation set until your metric improves.
Alex: Give me a real product that lives inside this workflow.
Jordan: Look at Gmail. Back in February twenty-nineteen, Google shared that TensorFlow-powered protections helped Gmail block around one hundred million extra spam messages every day alongside rules. They claimed it blocked twenty percent more spam and reviewed one thousand times more user reports daily.
Alex: That is Google's own shipped product claim, but the architecture illustrates the loop.
Jordan: Completely. The task is classifying inbound messages as wanted, spam, or phishing. The examples are historical emails, links, and user reports. The labels come from users clicking report spam or security analysts. They train models to catch patterns rules miss, evaluate them against held-out data using tools like TensorBoard, and deploy them with live feedback. The value is the engineered pipeline, not magic.
