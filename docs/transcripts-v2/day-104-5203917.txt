Alex: If you mix up what a model predicts with what a rule enforces, your automation is going to fail in expensive ways. This is AI Foundations, lesson 2.
Jordan: Let us start by untangling three words people constantly blur together: patterns, predictions, and rules.
Alex: Break down a pattern first.
Jordan: A pattern is just an observed regularity in data. It is not a law of physics. If you run a farm, you might notice diesel use spikes every harvest window. If you analyze commercial real estate, you might notice loans with weak debt-service coverage ratios run into trouble more often. That is a pattern. Machine learning is software spotting those regularities at scale.
Alex: But a pattern by itself does not make a decision.
Jordan: Right. That is where prediction comes in. A prediction is the model output for a new, unseen case. It takes that historical pattern and says, this incoming email is probably an invoice, or this tenant has an elevated renewal risk, or the next token in this sentence is probably therefore.
Alex: And it is probabilistic. It comes with a confidence score, not a guarantee.
Jordan: A prediction can be wrong, but it can still be valuable. If a model routes eighty out of a hundred routine emails correctly and leaves the uncertain twenty for a human, you just saved hours of work. But confidence is not truth. It is just an estimate based on training data.
Alex: So where does a rule fit in?
Jordan: A rule is an explicit instruction or constraint. If an email sender is bank dot com and the subject line says wire, send an alert immediately. Or never auto-pay an invoice over five thousand dollars without approval. Rules do not guess. They execute.
Alex: The trade-off is that rules are rigid. If an email says wire change instead of wire transfer, a simple rule might miss it completely.
Jordan: That is the tension. Rules are brittle when reality gets messy. Predictions are flexible, but they are uncertain. Strong automations combine both.
Alex: What about the word model? Where does that sit in the chain?
Jordan: The model is the mechanism that turns inputs into outputs. It could be a simple decision tree, or a massive neural network. Back in 2023, OpenAI published their GPT-4 technical report describing it as a transformer pre-trained to predict the next token, then shaped by safety and alignment training. That is all a model is. It learns patterns, outputs predictions, and gets constrained by rules.
Alex: And generalization is whether that model survives contact with the real world.
Jordan: Spot on. Generalization means the model performs well on brand new examples, not just the past cases it memorized. If your invoice classifier breaks the second a vendor sends a scanned PDF or a bilingual email, it did not generalize.
Alex: Your farm manager analogy fits this really well.
Jordan: Think of a farm manager walking a field. They look at tire tracks, soil moisture, and weather logs. Those are patterns. They predict whether the field is ready for harvest. That is a prediction. But they also follow hard rules about equipment weight limits and safety. The manager judgment is the model. The whole operating procedure is the automation.
Alex: You can see that exact architecture in Gmail spam filtering. Google blocks over ninety-nine point nine percent of spam and phishing, and they do not do it with just a raw neural network.
Jordan: Not even close. It is a textbook hybrid. The pattern layer observes large-scale signals like sender infrastructure, lookalike domains, and user complaints. The prediction layer scores incoming mail to estimate if a message is clean, spam, or phishing before it hits your inbox.
Alex: But the rule layer sets the hard boundaries.
Jordan: Exactly. Domain authentication, sandboxing suspicious attachments, policy settings from Workspace admins. You do not ask an AI to guess if a known piece of malware is safe. You make a rule that drops it immediately.
Alex: So for anyone building their own workflows, the blueprint is clear: rules for non-negotiables, predictions for fuzzy routing, and humans for the edge cases.
