Alex: You do not always need a massive supercomputer to get real work done with artificial intelligence. This is AI Foundations, day 62.
Jordan: Today is about making AI smaller, faster, and cheaper to run without gutting the quality of what it produces.
Alex: Which matters immediately if you are trying to deploy something on a smartphone, a cheap cloud instance, or anywhere with a strict budget.
Jordan: Exactly. When we talk about a small model, we mean one with fewer parameters. Parameters are the internal numbers a model learns during training. Fewer parameters mean the model demands less memory and compute when it runs inference.
Alex: And just to keep our terms straight, training is when the model learns from data, while inference is when we actually run the trained model to answer a prompt.
Jordan: Right. In production, inference is where your ongoing bills come from. Smaller models slash latency, boost throughput, and fit on constrained devices. But the real test is whether a smaller model is good enough for your specific task.
Alex: A tiny model might nail sentiment analysis, data extraction, or sorting emails, but fall flat on multi-step reasoning or complex multimodal inputs.
Jordan: Precisely. If a task is narrow, you usually do not need all that extra bulk.
Alex: So if we want a capable small model, how do we actually build one without losing all the smarts of a bigger system?
Jordan: One major technique is distillation. You take a massive, capable model as the teacher and train a smaller student model to mimic its outputs.
Alex: So the student is not trying to learn the whole world from raw data. It gets a guided curriculum.
Jordan: Exactly. Think of it like turning a giant eight-hundred-page textbook into a compact study guide written by an expert tutor. The study guide is shorter, easier to carry, and captures the core patterns. But it might skip some edge-case nuances.
Alex: Is there a catch with distillation?
Jordan: The student inherits the teacher's blind spots. If the teacher hallucinates, carries specific biases, or makes inconsistent errors, the student will faithfully copy those exact flaws.
Alex: That makes sense. Now, what about quantization? I hear people throwing that term around constantly whenever local models come up.
Jordan: Quantization changes how the model stores its numbers. Model weights are usually stored in high precision, like sixteen-bit numbers. Quantization rounds those numbers down to something smaller, like eight-bit values.
Alex: Like taking a detailed map with ten decimal places for every GPS coordinate and rounding them to two.
Jordan: That is the exact mental model. The map still gets you to the right street, but you lose sub-millimeter precision. That rounding introduces approximation error. It saves massive amounts of memory and speeds up calculations, but if you compress too aggressively, the model gets sloppy.
Alex: And we also hear about pruning. How does that fit in?
Jordan: Pruning is literally cutting out unimportant weights or connections entirely. Both pruning and quantization fall under the broad umbrella of model compression. But remember the distinction: distillation changes what the model learns during training, while quantization changes how the model stores and computes its numbers during inference.
Alex: How do these pieces fit into real systems alongside the caching and batching tricks we talked about recently?
Jordan: Let us look at a concrete setup. Say a company gets ten thousand customer support tickets a day. Most are dead simple: asking for an invoice, resetting a password, or canceling an account.
Alex: Running a massive frontier model on every single password reset request would burn money for no reason.
Jordan: Total waste. So they train a compact intent classification model. They distill it using a large model that already labeled historical tickets. Then they quantize that small model so it runs lightning fast on basic servers.
Alex: The small, quantized model handles the vast majority of routine routing in milliseconds.
Jordan: And if a ticket is strange, ambiguous, or angry, the system routes that single request up to a larger model or a human agent. You combine compression with model routing to get speed, low cost, and safety all at once.
Alex: It makes the entire architecture lean.
Jordan: Let us see how well you internalized this. First question: what is the fundamental difference between distillation and quantization?
Alex: Distillation changes the training process by having a smaller student model learn from a big teacher model. Quantization changes the numerical precision of the weights, like shrinking sixteen-bit numbers down to eight-bit, to save memory and compute during inference.
Jordan: Spot on. Second question: why would a company use a small model for customer support ticket routing instead of a giant general model?
Alex: Ticket classification is a narrow, repetitive task. A small model is faster, costs much less, runs on lighter hardware, and handles the high volume without sacrificing quality on simple queries.
Jordan: Exactly right. Last one: in what situation would model routing be better than just picking one single model for every request?
Alex: When your workload is a mix of easy and hard tasks. You let the cheap, fast model handle the simple eighty percent, and only pay the latency and cost penalty of a big model for the tricky edge cases.
