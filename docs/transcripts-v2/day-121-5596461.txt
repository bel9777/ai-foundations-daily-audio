Alex: There is a preprint from October 2026 on arXiv called "When Does Muon Actually Win?" The authors tested this newer optimizer, Muon, directly against the industry standard, AdamW, across five matched seeds.
Jordan: And the headline number looked great. Muon cut validation loss area under the curve by up to twenty-two percent when holding the training data constant.
Alex: But the paper's real point was about how we measure a win.
Jordan: Exactly. If you give both optimizers the exact same data, Muon often wins. But if you hold wall-clock time fixed, or if you account for the hours spent tuning hyperparameters, the gap shifts. Another benchmark from September showed AdamW is still a beast of a baseline across diffusion tasks. There is no silver bullet.
Alex: Every modern AI model exists because an algorithm figured out how to nudge billions of numbers until the errors shrank. This is AI Foundations, lesson 11.
Jordan: Today is about gradient descent: how we turn yesterday's loss score into actual weight updates.
Alex: Let's start with the landscape. We have weights, which are just adjustable numbers inside the model, and we have a loss function.
Jordan: Picture a massive terrain. Every point on the map is a specific combination of weights. The elevation at that point is your loss. If the model is making terrible predictions, you are high up on a mountain peak. Training is simply hiking down to the lowest valley you can find.
Alex: To hike down, you need to know which way is downhill. That is the gradient.
Jordan: Right. The gradient is a vector of partial derivatives. For every single weight, it asks: if I nudge this weight up just a tiny bit, does the overall loss go up or down, and by how much? Mathematically, the gradient points toward steepest ascent. So we just step in the exact opposite direction.
Alex: And in a neural net with billions of weights, backpropagation is just the accounting system using the calculus chain rule to calculate all those slopes backward in a single pass.
Jordan: Exactly. But knowing the direction is only half the battle. You also have to decide how big a step to take. That is your learning rate, or eta.
Alex: If your step is too small, you spend weeks crawling down a gentle slope.
Jordan: And if your step is too big, you launch yourself right over the valley floor, smash into the opposite canyon wall, and bounce out into the stratosphere. In your terminal, that shows up as loss spikes or NaN, not a number, because the values overflowed. That is why almost every major training run uses a learning rate schedule: a short warm-up so the model does not stumble early, followed by a steady decay.
Alex: Now, you cannot compute the true gradient across trillions of tokens every single step. That would take forever.
Jordan: You cannot. So you estimate it using a small random batch of data. That is mini-batch stochastic gradient descent. It is cheap, and while the estimate is noisy, that noise is actually a feature. It jiggles the model out of shallow, bad pockets and pushes it toward wider valleys that generalize better to new data.
Alex: You had a great fog analogy for this.
Jordan: Imagine you are stuck in a field in pitch-black darkness and dense fog, trying to find the lowest puddle. You cannot see the field. You can only feel the tilt of the ground right under your boots. That tilt is your gradient. Your stride length is the learning rate. Feeling the slope at just a few random spots before each stride is your mini-batch.
Alex: And what about momentum?
Jordan: Momentum means if you have been running downhill for five steps, you keep rolling forward so a small tractor rut does not stop you in your tracks.
Alex: Where do AdamW and Muon fit into that foggy field?
Jordan: Adam and AdamW give every single weight its own personalized stride length based on how wild its past gradients were, while applying weight decay cleanly. Muon goes a step further. Instead of treating each weight separately, it treats an entire two-dimensional weight matrix as a single geometric object. It orthogonalizes the update, which basically rescales the step so the model pushes evenly in all directions rather than slipping sideways along deep ruts.
Alex: And we are already seeing this in frontier systems. DeepSeek-V4 shipped as a massive mixture-of-experts model, and they switched most of their parameter updates from AdamW over to Muon.
Jordan: Then in V4.1-Flash, they introduced head-wise Muon, splitting attention query matrices by head before orthogonalizing them. But here is the catch: Muon only works on two-dimensional matrices. So modern setups are hybrids. PyTorch ships torch dot optim dot Muon, and the docs explicitly tell you to use AdamW for embeddings, biases, and non-two-D layers, while running Muon on the big projection matrices.
Alex: And Moonshot AI used something called MuonClip on Kimi K2 to keep those giant steps from blowing up the logits.
Jordan: Precisely. High-stakes scale requires guardrails against overshooting. Let's see how well you can troubleshoot these dynamics. First question: your nightly forecasting model logs loss dropping smoothly from zero-point-nine to zero-point-four, but suddenly it jumps to three-point-seven, and then step three hundred hits NaN. The only change was a colleague bumped the learning rate from ten to the minus three up to ten to the minus two. What happened, and give me two fixes besides reverting it.
Alex: The step size became way too large. The model overshot the valley, hit a steep wall, calculated an even bigger gradient, and cascaded until the floating-point math overflowed into NaN. To fix it, you could add a learning rate schedule with a warm-up and decay, or implement gradient clipping to cap the maximum step size.
Jordan: Spot on. You could also check if your input features were properly normalized, or switch to an adaptive optimizer like AdamW. Question two: you retrain an invoice detector monthly. Training loss goes down every month, but out in production, the percentage of real exceptions it catches is dropping. What does gradient descent know about that, and what do you change?
Alex: Gradient descent knows literally nothing about future production data. It only blindly minimizes error on the training set you feed it. That divergence means you are overfitting or the real-world invoice distribution drifted. To fix it, set up early stopping on a held-out validation set from the most recent month, add weight decay, and track actual business metrics like recall rather than just relying on loss.
