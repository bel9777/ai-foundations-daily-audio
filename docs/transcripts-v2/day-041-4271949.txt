Alex: If an AI sounds completely confident, how do you know it isn't just making things up? This is AI Foundations, day 41.
Jordan: That is the big problem with language models. They write fluent, believable text whether they are looking at real facts or completely hallucinating.
Alex: Right, so today we are talking about grounding. What does that actually mean in practice?
Jordan: Grounding just means connecting the AI's answer directly to verifiable evidence, like a document, a spreadsheet row, or a meeting note. Think of it like a museum audio guide with exhibit labels. The guide can tell a great story about the painting, but it needs to point you to the actual canvas on the wall so you can see it for yourself.
Alex: Without the label, you are just taking the audio guide's word for it. With the label, you can check the canvas.
Jordan: Exactly. Grounding isn't about making the model infallible. It's about making its answers traceable. That requires two things: giving the answer, and showing the evidence that backs it up.
Alex: Okay, but what counts as evidence?
Jordan: Anything concrete you provided in the context—a specific paragraph, a log entry, or a policy page. And to show that evidence, you want citations and quotations.
Alex: I know what those are in academic writing, but how do they work in AI workflows?
Jordan: A citation tells you where the fact came from, like Policy version two, section four. A quotation gives you the exact words copied from that source.
Alex: So a citation points to the map, but a quote shows you the actual footprint.
Jordan: Spot on. You can have a citation without a quote, but asking for both gives you way higher confidence that the model didn't misinterpret the source.
Alex: That brings up a sticky point, though. What if the AI takes source facts and draws a logical conclusion from them? Is that allowed?
Jordan: It is allowed, but it has to be explicitly labeled. We divide statements into attributable and inferred. Attributable means the document explicitly says it. Inferred means the model drew a conclusion.
Alex: Give me an example.
Jordan: Say a store policy says refunds are allowed within thirty days. That is attributable. If the model says a request on day thirty-one will likely be denied, that is inferred.
Alex: The source didn't explicitly mention day thirty-one, even if the logic holds up.
Jordan: Exactly. If you blend those together, users cannot tell what came from the official rules and what came from the AI's interpretation. Always report source facts first, then label your inferences clearly.
Alex: Which brings us to faithful summaries. Is a faithful summary just a strict paraphrase?
Jordan: A faithful summary stays strictly inside the guardrails of the text. No made-up additions, no hidden assumptions, and no unasked-for extra advice. This is where breaking down tasks through decomposition comes back in. You tell the model to find the relevant source, extract key lines, and summarize only those lines.
Alex: How does this connect to what we learned earlier about RAG and reranking?
Jordan: Retrieval-augmented generation retrieves the relevant documents and puts them in front of the model. Then search and reranking pick the highest quality chunks. Grounding is what happens next. Retrieval brings evidence to the table; grounding makes sure the model actually uses it and displays it clearly in the answer.
Alex: If retrieval pulls the wrong chunk, even a faithful summary will be grounded in the wrong evidence.
Jordan: Right. Garbage retrieved, grounded garbage delivered.
Alex: So how do we actually force an AI to behave this way when we write a prompt?
Jordan: You give it strict guardrails. Tell it to answer using only the provided sources, demand citations and short quotes for every claim, instruct it to say "not supported by sources" if evidence is missing, and separate facts from inferences.
Alex: Can we walk through a real-world example?
Jordan: Imagine a customer refund policy that says: Customers may request a refund within thirty days of purchase. Refunds are not available for digital products after download.
Alex: How should a grounded response look?
Jordan: First, the concise answer: Refunds are available within thirty days, but digital products cannot be refunded after download. Second, the evidence lines: Citation, Refund Policy section two, with the exact quoted sentence. Third, another evidence line quoting the digital product rule. Finally, an inference line: Inference, if a customer downloaded a digital file, the policy does not support a refund request.
Alex: That is super clean. Brief answer, quoted evidence, labeled inference. Anyone reading that can audit it in five seconds.

Alex: A citation tells you where the information came from, like a file name or section number. A quotation gives you the source's exact words copied word-for-word.
Jordan: Perfect. Question two: Why should source facts and model inferences be separated in a grounded answer?
Alex: So readers can tell what is directly supported by the source document versus what is just the model's interpretation or conclusion.
Jordan: Right on. Question three: In a grounded workflow, what role do retrieval and reranking play before the answer is written?
Alex: Retrieval finds candidate source material, and reranking helps select the most relevant chunks so the answer can be grounded in the absolute best evidence available.
